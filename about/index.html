A reference you can check

A reference of definitions, theorems and solved problems from economic theory, each one stated precisely and checked by computer in Lean 4.

How every entry is made The team How to cite Updated 18 October 2026

Formal Economics collects the definitions, theorems and problems of economic theory — today a textbook read in full, a PhD course, a qualifying exam and papers — and states each one precisely, the way a good textbook would. Behind every statement is a proof in Lean 4, a proof assistant, which the computer checks line by line.

You do not need to read Lean to use it. Each entry gives the statement, its exact assumptions, an intuition, where it comes from and how it connects to the rest. The Lean statement stands beside it, for anyone who wants the guarantee itself.

How every entry is made

Behind every entry stand its theorems and definitions in Lean, and every line of them is checked.

See it on one theorem →
  1. 1

    State it as the source does.

    Each definition, theorem and problem is written out in the economist’s language, from the page where the source states it: a textbook, a course, an exam or a paper.

  2. 2

    State it in Lean.

    The same statement is written in Lean 4, a language in which mathematics can be checked by computer, and proved there. The computer verifies every step of the proof.

  3. 3

    Align the two.

    Each assumption of the Lean statement is matched, one by one, with an assumption the source states, so that nothing is quietly added, dropped or strengthened.

  4. 4

    Keep the printed word.

    The printed sentence is kept word for word; where a condition is needed, a counterexample shows why, and the statement with the condition is proved beside it.

What the marks mean

Proved as printed
Stated as the source states it, and proved by computer.

+Added condition

A topic’s icon
Every topic of the handbook has its own icon: a small, exact diagram of its economics, shown beside its entries. It identifies the topic at a glance; the statement is the guarantee.

How to cite

Cite an entry by its permanent tag, shown at the top of every entry: Formal Economics, entry M7M6. A tag never changes. Each entry’s page gives BibTeX for the entry and for its source; to cite the library as a whole, use the record beside.

BibTeX 
@misc{formaleconomics,
  title        = {{Formal Economics}: Economic theory, machine-checked},
  author       = {Ruibo Song and Ce Li and Dirk Bergemann and Ethan X. Fang and Junwei Lu and Zhuoran Yang},
  howpublished = {\url{https://formal-economics.github.io/}},
  year         = {2026}
}

How to take part

  • Ask for a result.

    Missing a theorem you need, a paper you teach, or a problem set? Write to a member of the team, through the pages linked above, and name the source and the result as you would cite them. Requests from economists decide what comes next.

  • Report a slip.

    If an entry’s wording, a locator or a page number is wrong, tell us which entry, by its tag. The mathematics is checked by computer; the prose around it is checked by people.

  • Use the data.

    The catalogue is open data: every entry, with its statements and assumptions, in one JSON file for research, teaching or your own tools. Download the library.

  • Follow the work.

    Kamenica and Gentzkow (2011), Kahneman and Tversky (1979) and Holmström (1979) are being formalized now; their results appear in the bibliography as they are checked.
