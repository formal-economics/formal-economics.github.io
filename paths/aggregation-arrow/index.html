Aggregation: from majority rule to Arrow and Gibbard–Satterthwaite

Why majority rule works for two alternatives, and why with three or more Pareto and IIA leave only dictators

Begin with step 1 → First-year PhDAbout 75 minutes

The route

  1. Social welfare functions
  2. May’s theorem
  3. Pareto condition (Par)
  4. Independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA)
  5. Decisive coalitions and Arrow’s theorem
  6. Arrow’s impossibility theorem
  7. Independence of the conditions of Arrow’s theorem
  8. Borda rules violate IIA
  9. Majority rule with single peaks
  10. Who is an economist?

The guide assembles the impossibility from its ingredients and proves it, then tests each condition, looks for a way out, and ends with the version for strategic voters.

  1. 1

    Rubinstein, Lecture 9, p. 129

    Step 1: Social welfare functions

    Most of what follows is a statement about rules of this kind: constitutions that turn individual opinions into a ranking for society.

    Read the definition

    A model of social choice consists of

    • a set X of social alternatives;
    • a finite set N of individuals, with n=∣N∣;
    • a social welfare function (SWF) F that assigns to every profile (≻1​,…,≻n​), which lists one ordering for each individual (a preference relation with no indifference between distinct alternatives), a preference relation F(≻1​,…,≻n​) on X: complete, reflexive and transitive, with indifference allowed.
  2. 2

    Rubinstein, Problem Set 9, Problem 2, p. 136

    Step 2: May’s theorem

    Before the impossibility, the good news: with only two alternatives, majority rule is singled out by three appealing axioms.

    Read the question

    This problem follows May (1952). Society faces just two alternatives in X. A social welfare function (SWF) maps each profile of preferences to a preference; both individuals and society may be indifferent. The axioms are:

    • Anonymity: take any permutation σ of N; whenever two profiles p={≿i​} and p′={≿i′​} are related by ≿σ(i)′​=≿i​, we have ≿(p)=≿(p′).
    • Neutrality: when each individual’s preference is flipped, the social preference flips too.
    • Positive responsiveness: suppose x≿y under p, and p′ is obtained from p by changing a single individual j, either from x∼j​y to x≻j′​y, or from y≻j​x to x∼j′​y. Then under p′ we get x≻′y.

    (a) Explain what each axiom means. (b) Verify that majority rule meets all three. (c) Establish May’s theorem: no SWF other than majority rule meets them. (d) Is each axiom independent of the other two?

  3. 3

    Rubinstein, Lecture 9, p. 130

    Step 3: Pareto condition (Par)

    The first of Arrow’s two conditions, a minimal requirement that few would give up.

    Read the definition

    An SWF F satisfies Par if for all x,y∈X and every profile (≻i​)i∈N​: if x≻i​y for every i, then x≻y, where ≻ is the strict part of the social preference F(≻1​,…,≻n​).

  4. 4

    Rubinstein, Lecture 9, pp. 130–131

    Step 4: Independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA)

    The second condition, and the more demanding: together with Pareto and a third alternative, it lets power over one pair of alternatives spread to every other pair.

    Read the definition

    An SWF F satisfies IIA if for all x,y∈X and any two profiles P=(≻i​) and P′=(⊳i​): if x≻i​y⟺x⊳i​y for every i, then x≿y⟺x⊵y, where ≿=F(P) and ⊵=F(P′).

  5. 5

    Rubinstein, Problem Set 9, Problem 5, p. 137

    Step 5: Decisive coalitions and Arrow’s theorem

    The engine of the proof, in small lemmas: with at least three alternatives, power shrinks from a group to a single individual.

    Read the question

    Decisive and almost decisive coalitions appear in several proofs of Arrow’s theorem. Fix an SWF. Call a coalition G decisive for x,y when [x≻i​y for all i∈G] forces [x≻y]; call it almost decisive for x,y when [x≻i​y for all i∈G and y≻j​x for all j∈/G] forces [x≻y]. Decisiveness for x,y implies almost decisiveness for x,y, and G is called decisive when it is decisive for every pair x,y. Assume F is an SWF meeting Par and IIA.

    • (a) Establish the Field Expansion Lemma: when G is almost decisive for x,y, it is decisive for x,z and also for y,z, whatever z∈/{x,y} is chosen.
    • (b) Prove that a coalition G almost decisive for x,y is decisive.
    • (c) Establish the Group Contraction Lemma: when G is decisive with ∣G∣≥2, there is a decisive G′⊂G.
    • (d) Prove that {i∗} is decisive for some individual i∗.
  6. 6

    Rubinstein, Lecture 9, p. 131

    Step 6: Arrow’s impossibility theorem

    The theorem itself, which now follows quickly: the individual found in the previous step is a dictator.

    Read the claim

    Let N be a finite set of individuals and let X contain at least three alternatives. If a social welfare function F satisfies Par and IIA, then F is dictatorial: there is an individual i∗ such that for every profile and all x,y∈X, society strictly prefers x to y if and only if x≻i∗​y.

  7. 7

    Rubinstein, Lecture 9, p. 131

    Step 7: Independence of the conditions of Arrow’s theorem

    Each ingredient earns its place: drop Pareto, the third alternative or transitivity of the social ranking, and rules without a dictator appear.

    Read the claim
    1. (Par.) For any individual i0​ and ∣X∣≥3, the anti-dictatorial SWF, whose social preference is the reverse of i0​’s ordering, satisfies IIA, is not dictatorial, and violates Par.
    2. (∣X∣≥3.) If X has exactly two alternatives and N is finite with at least two individuals, there is an SWF that satisfies Par and IIA and is not dictatorial; the majority rule is one.
    3. With a single individual, no SWF satisfies Par and IIA without being dictatorial.
    Try it
  8. 8

    Rubinstein, Problem Set 9, Problem 1, p. 136

    Step 8: Borda rules violate IIA

    A popular family of rules through Arrow’s lens: Borda rules keep transitivity and Pareto, so it is IIA they must give up.

    Read the question

    Suppose there are two or more individuals and three or more alternatives. Show that IIA fails for every Borda rule.

  9. 9

    Rubinstein, Problem Set 9, Problem 3, p. 136

    Step 9: Majority rule with single peaks

    A way out that keeps majority rule: restrict which preferences are allowed, and Condorcet’s cycle disappears.

    Read the question

    Take X=[0,1] and suppose each person’s preference is single-peaked: person i has some alternative ai∗​ with b≻i​c in case ai∗​≥b>c or c>b≥ai∗​. Restrict the domain to such preferences. Prove that, when n is odd, majority rule yields a well-behaved social welfare function.

  10. 10

    Rubinstein, Problem Set 9, Problem 6, p. 138

    Step 10: Who is an economist?

    The same logic far from voting: deciding who counts as an economist from the faculty’s own views leads to a dictator as well.

    Read the question

    This problem follows Kasher and Rubinstein (1997). Who counts as an economist? View this as pooling the opinions of a department’s members. The faculty is N={1,…,n} with n≥3. Member i∈N reports a set Ei​ satisfying ∅⊂Ei​⊂N, namely those he regards as “real economists”. An aggregation method F sends every profile (E1​,…,En​) to a proper nonempty subset of N, read as the members the group regards as economists. The axioms:

    • Consensus: whenever j∈Ei​ for every i∈N, also j∈F(E1​,…,En​); whenever j∈/Ei​ for every i∈N, also j∈/F(E1​,…,En​).
    • Independence: whenever two profiles (E1​,…,En​) and (G1​,…,Gn​) satisfy [j∈Ei​⟺j∈Gi​] for every i, we have [j∈F(E1​,…,En​)⟺j∈F(G1​,…,Gn​)].

    (a) Explain what the two axioms say. (b) Give a method meeting Consensus but violating Independence, and a second method meeting Independence but violating Consensus. (c) (Difficult) Following the approach of Arrow’s impossibility theorem, prove that a method meets both axioms only if there is a member i∗ with F(E1​,…,En​)≡Ei∗​.

  11. 11

    Rubinstein, Lecture 9, pp. 133–134

    Step 11: Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem

    The destination: when voters can misreport, a rule that no one can ever manipulate, with at least three possible outcomes, must again be a dictatorship.

    Read the theorem
    1. A social choice function (SCF) C assigns an alternative C(≻1​,…,≻n​)∈X to every profile.
    2. C is straightforward if no individual ever gains by misrepresenting his preferences: for every profile, every individual i and every report ≻i′​, it is not the case that C(≻1​,…,≻i′​,…,≻n​)≻i​C(≻1​,…,≻i​,…,≻n​).
    3. Let Ch assign a choice function to every profile. Ch satisfies unanimity if for every profile, every choice set A and all x,y∈A: if y≻i​x for every i, then x is not chosen from A.
    4. Ch is invariant to the procedure if, for every profile and every choice set A split into two nonempty disjoint sets A′ and A′′, choosing from A′ and from A′′ and then choosing between the two picks gives the same outcome as choosing from A directly.
