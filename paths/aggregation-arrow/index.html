Aggregation: from majority rule to Arrow and Gibbard–Satterthwaite

Why majority rule works for two alternatives, and why with three or more Pareto and IIA leave only dictators

With two alternatives, majority rule is characterized by three natural axioms. With three or more, majority rule can be intransitive, and Arrow’s theorem shows that every social welfare function satisfying Pareto and independence of irrelevant alternatives is dictatorial. The path follows the proof, the role of each condition, one way out through single-peaked preferences, and the strategic version of the result.

  1. 1

    Definition · Rubinstein, Lecture 9, p. 129

    Social welfare functions

    A social welfare function assigns to every profile of individual orderings a social preference relation: complete, reflexive and transitive, with indifference allowed.

    Definition

    A model of social choice consists of

    • a set X of social alternatives;
    • a finite set N of individuals, with n=∣N∣;
    • a social welfare function (SWF) F that assigns to every profile (≻1​,…,≻n​), which lists one ordering for each individual (a preference relation with no indifference between distinct alternatives), a preference relation F(≻1​,…,≻n​) on X: complete, reflexive and transitive, with indifference allowed.

    May’s theorem

    May’s theorem: with two alternatives, and indifference allowed, a social welfare function is anonymous, neutral and positively responsive exactly when it is majority rule; none of the three axioms follows from the other two.

    Definition · Rubinstein, Lecture 9, p. 130

    Pareto condition (Par)

    Pareto: if every individual strictly prefers x to y, so does society.

    Definition

    An SWF F satisfies Par if for all x,y∈X and every profile (≻i​)i∈N​: if x≻i​y for every i, then x≻y, where ≻ is the strict part of the social preference F(≻1​,…,≻n​).

  5. 5

    Problem · Rubinstein, Problem Set 9, Problem 5, p. 137

    Decisive coalitions and Arrow’s theorem

    The engine of the proof: under Pareto and IIA, with at least three alternatives, a coalition almost decisive for one pair of distinct alternatives is decisive for every pair, every decisive coalition with two or more members contains a smaller decisive one, and so some single individual is decisive. As printed, the problem requires neither distinct alternatives nor a third one; examples show that both are needed.

    Claim · Rubinstein, Lecture 9, p. 131

    Arrow’s impossibility theorem

    Arrow’s impossibility theorem: with finitely many individuals and at least three alternatives, every social welfare function satisfying Pareto and IIA is dictatorial. The decisive individual of the previous step is the dictator.

  7. 7

    Claim · Rubinstein, Lecture 9, p. 131

    Independence of the conditions of Arrow’s theorem

    Each condition matters: with three or more alternatives, the rule that reverses one individual’s ordering satisfies IIA and is not dictatorial, but violates Pareto; with two alternatives, majority rule satisfies Pareto and IIA without a dictator; and with three or more it satisfies both but can be intransitive, Condorcet’s paradox. The printed majority-rule examples state no bound on the number of individuals; the machine-checked versions assume at least two.

    Majority rule with single peaks

    Who is an economist?

    The same logic in another problem (Kasher and Rubinstein): when each member of a faculty of at least three names some but not all members as economists, and the faculty’s verdict must do the same, Consensus and Independence leave only the methods that always adopt one member’s view.

  11. 11

    Theorem · Rubinstein, Lecture 9, pp. 133–134

    Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem

    Gibbard–Satterthwaite: with at least three alternatives, a straightforward (strategy-proof) social choice function onto X always picks one fixed individual’s favourite; Muller–Satterthwaite and Dutta–Jackson–Le Breton give the related results for monotone welfare functions and procedure-invariant choice rules. As printed, the three carry no condition on the number of alternatives and fail with two; the machine-checked versions assume at least three, among other conditions.
