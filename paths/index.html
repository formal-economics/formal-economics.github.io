Reading paths

7 guided routes through the handbook. Each takes one question of economic theory from its definitions to its landmark results, one entry at a time, with a sentence on why each step comes next.

78 steps about 8 hours of readingOr browse by topic
  1. 1

    From preferences to utility: the road to Debreu

    When a ranking can be written as a number, and when that number can be continuous

    11 steps · about 50 minutes · advanced undergraduate · Preferences, Utility representation

    Preference relation → Utility representation → Utility is ordinal → Utility representation on finite sets → Utility on countable sets → Integer-valued utility on a countable set → The lexicographic preference on the unit square → The lexicographic counterexample → Continuity of preferences (open-ball form) → Lexicographic preferences have no utility representation → Debreu’s theorem

  2. 2

    Rational choice: from choice functions to the weak axiom

    When can observed choices be explained by maximizing one preference?

    12 steps · about 45 minutes · advanced undergraduate · Choice and rationalizability

    Choice function → Rational choice function → Rationalizable choice → Condition α → Rational choice satisfies condition α → Rationalizability of choice functions satisfying condition α → Choice correspondence → Weak axiom of revealed preference (choice correspondences) → Condition α (choice correspondences) → Condition β (choice correspondences) → Rationalizability by α and β → The weak axiom (WARP)

  3. 3

    The consumer: from the budget set to Roy’s identity and duality

    Existence and shape of demand, indirect utility, expenditure, and the duality between them

    12 steps · about 70 minutes · first-year PhD · Consumer demand and duality, Consumer preferences

    Monotone preferences → Existence of demand → Uniqueness of demand → Walras’ law → MRS equals the price ratio → Indirect preferences over budget sets → Roy’s identity → Is v = w/p₁ + w/p₂ a rational consumer’s indirect utility? → Hicksian demand and the expenditure function → Properties of Hicksian demand and the expenditure function → Consumer duality → Recovering utility from the expenditure function

  4. 4

    The firm: from technologies to Hotelling’s and Shephard’s lemmas

    Technologies and production functions, profit and supply, cost, and comparative statics without calculus

    10 steps · about 55 minutes · first-year PhD · Producers and the firm

    Technology → Production functions and technologies → Convex technologies and concave production functions → Profit maximization: profit and supply functions → Properties of the supply function → Hotelling’s lemma → The cost function → Shephard’s lemma → A monopolist’s output never rises with marginal cost → Skill and the choice of technology

  5. 5

    Decisions under risk: from lotteries to expected utility and Arrow–Pratt

    Independence, the expected utility theorem, and risk attitudes read off the utility of money

    11 steps · about 80 minutes · first-year PhD · Choice under uncertainty, Risk attitudes

    Monotonicity lemma for the independence axiom → Expected utility theorem → Mean–variance preferences and expected utility → Betweenness and the Allais paradox → First-order stochastic dominance → Risk aversion and concavity → How much insurance to buy → Second-order stochastic dominance → Arrow–Pratt → Constant absolute risk aversion → Who pays more for information?

  6. 6

    Aggregation: from majority rule to Arrow and Gibbard–Satterthwaite

    Why majority rule works for two alternatives, and why with three or more Pareto and IIA leave only dictators

    11 steps · about 75 minutes · first-year PhD · Social choice

    Social welfare functions → May’s theorem → Pareto condition (Par) → Independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) → Decisive coalitions and Arrow’s theorem → Arrow’s impossibility theorem → Independence of the conditions of Arrow’s theorem → Borda rules violate IIA → Majority rule with single peaks → Who is an economist? → Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem

  7. 7

    Is it rational? Worked practice from the problem sets to a qualifying exam

    Choice procedures, demand patterns and aggregate demand, each tested against the maximization of a preference

    11 steps · about 90 minutes · first-year PhD · Choice and rationalizability, Behavioral decision theory, Consumer demand and duality

    Satisficing is rationalizable → Is the procedure rational? → Choosing from the most common class → Threshold procedures (Kalai, Rubinstein and Spiegler) → Lexicographically rational choice (Rubinstein and Salant) → Choice by rationales (Cherepanov, Feddersen and Sandroni) → Luce’s choice axiom → Which behaviour patterns are rationalizable? → Giffen goods are inferior → An economy with no positive representative consumer → Demand systems and expected utility
