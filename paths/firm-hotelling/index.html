The firm: from technologies to Hotelling’s lemma and comparative statics

Technologies and production functions, profit and supply, cost, and comparative statics without calculus

Begin with step 1 → First-year PhDAbout 45 minutes

The route

  1. Technology
  2. Production functions and technologies

  5. Hotelling’s lemma
  6. The cost function
  7. A monopolist’s output never rises with marginal cost

The guide describes a firm by what it can make, then lets it maximize profit and asks what output costs. Two arguments do most of the work. One compares two optimal plans, each at its own prices; the other notes that the profit function touches a plane at the optimum and stays on one side of it, so where it is differentiable, its slope is the supply.

  1. 1

    Rubinstein, Lecture 6, pp. 88–89

    Step 1: Technology

    The firm’s primitive, playing the role preferences played for the consumer; every later object, from supply to cost, is computed from it.

    Read the definition

    A technology is a set Z⊆RK of production combinations; positive components are outputs and negative components inputs. Conditions that may be imposed:

    1. 0∈Z: inaction is possible;
    2. no production without resources: no z∈Z∩R+K​ other than 0;
    3. free disposal: z∈Z and z′≤z imply z′∈Z;
    4. Z is closed;
    5. Z is convex.

    If Z is convex and 0∈Z, then λz∈Z for every z∈Z and every λ∈[0,1).

  2. 2

    Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 89

    Step 2: Production functions and technologies

    With a single output, the same producer is often easier to describe by a function, and this step shows how to pass between the two descriptions.

    Read the definition

    Consider the single-output case: commodity K is produced from commodities 1,…,K−1, i.e. every z∈Z has zK​≥0 and zk​≤0 for k=K.

    1. From a technology Z, the production function is f(v)=max{x:(−v,x)∈Z} for input vectors v∈R+K−1​.
    2. From a production function f, the derived “technology” is Z(f)={(−w,x):x≤f(v) and w≥v for some v∈R+K−1​}.
  3. 3

    Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 91

    Step 3: Profit maximization: profit and supply functions

    Prices enter, and with them the two objects the next steps study: the most a firm can earn, and the plan that earns it.

    Read the definition

    Let Z⊆RK be a technology and p∈RK a price vector.

    1. A plan z solves the producer’s problem maxz∈Z​p⋅z if z∈Z and p⋅z′≤p⋅z for all z′∈Z.
    2. The profit function is π(p)=maxz∈Z​p⋅z.
    3. When the producer’s problem has a unique solution, that solution is the supply function z(p).
  4. 4

    Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 92

    Step 4: Properties of the supply function

    Before any derivative appears, supply already has a direction: the law of supply follows from comparing two plans, each at its own prices.

    Read the claim

    Let Z⊆RK be a technology. Each statement assumes that the producer’s problems it mentions have unique solutions.

    1. z(λp)=z(p) for every λ>0.
    2. If Z is closed, convex and bounded above (there is B with zk​≤B for all z∈Z and all k), then z is continuous wherever it is defined.
    3. (Law of supply.) If z(p)=z(p′), then (p−p′)⋅(z(p)−z(p′))>0. In particular, if p and p′ differ only in coordinate k, with pk​>pk′​, and z(p)=z(p′), then zk​(p)>zk​(p′).
  5. 5

    Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 92

    Step 5: Hotelling’s lemma

    The lemma in the guide’s title, and the reason the profit function matters: it carries the whole supply in its slopes.

    Read the claim

    Let Z⊆RK be a technology with profit function π and supply function z. Each statement assumes that the maxima and unique solutions it mentions exist.

    1. π(λp)=λπ(p) for every λ>0.
    2. If Z is closed, convex and bounded above, π is continuous wherever the supply is defined.
    3. π is convex: π(λp+(1−λ)p′)≤λπ(p)+(1−λ)π(p′) for λ∈[0,1].
    4. (Hotelling’s lemma.) p⋅z(p∗)≤π(p) for every p, with equality at p=p∗, so the hyperplane {(p,π):π=p⋅z(p∗)} supports the graph of π at p∗. If π is differentiable, and the maximum defining it is attained at all prices near p∗, then ∂π/∂pk​(p∗)=zk​(p∗).
    5. If π is twice continuously differentiable, its mixed second derivatives are symmetric: ∂2π/∂pk​∂pj​(p∗)=∂2π/∂pj​∂pk​(p∗). The book combines this with Hotelling’s lemma to conclude ∂zj​/∂pk​(p∗)=∂zk​/∂pj​(p∗).
  6. 6

    Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 92

    Step 6: The cost function

    From profit to cost: fix the output and ask what producing it must cost, which needs no output prices at all.

    Read the definition

    Let Z⊆RK be a technology whose commodities are L inputs followed by K−L outputs. For input prices p∈R++L​ and an output combination y∈R+K−L​, the cost function is

    c(p,y)=amin​{p⋅a:(−a,y)∈Z}.
  7. 7

    MIT 14.121, Problem Set 5, Question 4

    Step 7: A monopolist’s output never rises with marginal cost

    Comparative statics in the style of the law of supply: for a monopolist with a constant cost per unit, comparing two optimal outputs, each at its own cost, shows that output never rises with the cost, whatever the demand curve.

    Read the question

    Consider a monopolist with cost function c(q)=cq. Use a simple revealed preference argument to show that the monopolist’s output is weakly decreasing in c. (One can do this very generally, without making any assumptions about the demand curve.)

  8. 8

    MIT 14.121, Problem Set 6, Question 3

    Step 8: Skill and the choice of technology

    The destination takes the same idea further: whether the new technology raises the skill a firm hires turns on increasing differences, not on derivatives.

    Read the question

    Suppose there are two inputs to production, technology and the skill level of a single worker who uses the technology. With the computer technology the production function is G(s); with the old-fashioned technology it is H(s). The total cost of skill is c(s) on the market.

    • (a) What are sufficient conditions on G and H to ensure that profit-maximizing firms who use the computer-driven technology buy more skill than firms who use the old-fashioned technology? If we wish the result to hold without any structure on c(s), is there a weaker condition which will do? (Hint: define f(s,θ) with f(s,θH​)=G(s) and f(s,θL​)=H(s).)
    • (b) Suppose that the cost of the computer-driven technology falls smoothly over time, starting at a very high level and eventually reaching zero, and that firms choose their technology and level of skill at the same time. Assume whatever conditions you derived in part (a). Do we expect the skill level selected by the firms to change smoothly?
