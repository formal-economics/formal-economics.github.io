The consumer: from the budget set to Roy’s identity and duality

Existence and shape of demand, indirect utility, expenditure, and the duality between them

The consumer picks a best bundle from the budget set B(p,w)={x∈R+K:p⋅x≤w}B(p,w)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^K_+ : p\cdot x\le w\}B(p,w)={x∈R+K​:p⋅x≤w}. The guide follows that choice from its existence and shape to the value of a budget, then turns the problem around, to the cheapest way of reaching a given satisfaction; two worked problems along the way recover the same utility, one from each side.

  1. 1

    Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 49

    Step 1: Monotone preferences

    The alternatives are now bundles of goods. This assumption, that more of everything is welcome, is the one several later steps rely on, starting with Walras’ law.

    Read the definition

    A preference relation ≿ on the commodity space X=R+K​ is monotone if for all bundles x,y∈X:

    • if xk​≥yk​ for every k, then x≿y;
    • if xk​>yk​ for every k, then x≻y.
    Try it
  2. 2

    Rubinstein, Lecture 5, p. 67

    Step 2: Existence of demand

    Before describing the consumer’s choice, the guide makes sure there is one; the proof goes through Debreu’s theorem and the compactness of the budget set.

    Read the claim

    Let ≿ be a continuous preference relation on X=R+K​. Then for every price vector p with pk​>0 for all k and every wealth w>0, the budget set

    B(p,w)={x∈X:p⋅x≤w}

    contains a bundle x∗ with x∗≿y for every y∈B(p,w).

  3. 3

    Rubinstein, Lecture 5, p. 67

    Step 3: Uniqueness of demand

    With existence settled, the next question is how many best bundles there are: for a continuous, strictly convex preference exactly one, so demand becomes a function.

    Read the claim

    Let ≿ be a preference relation on X=R+K​, and let pk​>0 for all k and w>0.

    1. If ≿ is convex, the set of solutions of the consumer problem at (p,w) is convex. More generally, for every convex set A of bundles, the set of best elements of A under ≿ is convex.
    2. If ≿ is strictly convex, the consumer problem at (p,w) has at most one solution.
  4. 4

    Rubinstein, Lecture 5, p. 70

    Step 4: Walras’ law

    A property of demand that the rest of the guide leans on, from Roy’s identity to duality: no money is left on the table.

    Read the claim

    Let ≿ be a monotone preference relation on X=R+K​, and let pk​>0 for all k and w>0. If x solves the consumer problem at (p,w), then p⋅x=w.

  5. 5

    Rubinstein, Lecture 5, p. 69

    Step 5: MRS equals the price ratio

    Walras’ law puts the best bundle on the budget line; the textbook tangency condition says what must hold there between tastes and prices, for any two goods actually consumed.

    Read the claim

    Let ≿ be a differentiable preference relation on X=R+K​ with subjective value function v, so that D≿​(x)=Dv​(x) for every x; let pk​>0 for all k, w>0, and let x∗ solve the consumer problem at (p,w). If xk∗​>0 and xj∗​>0, then vk​(x∗)pj​=pk​vj​(x∗); when vj​(x∗)=0 this is the ratio condition

    vj​(x∗)vk​(x∗)​=pj​pk​​.
    Try it
  6. 6

    Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 83

    Step 6: Indirect preferences over budget sets

    The guide now turns from the bundle to the budget, asking how a consumer ranks different prices and wealth; this is the object Roy’s identity is about.

    Read the definition

    Let ≿ be a preference relation on X=R+K​ and x(p,w) a demand function. The indirect preference over price–wealth pairs is defined by

    ​(p,w)≿∗(p′,w′)⟺x(p,w)≿x(p′,w′).​
  7. 7

    Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 85

    Step 7: Roy’s identity

    The payoff of that turn: demand can be read off the derivatives of the indirect utility function, without solving the consumer’s problem again.

    Read the claim

    Let ≿ be a monotone preference relation on X=R+K​, let x(p,w) be a best bundle of B(p,w) under ≿ for every p≫0 and w>0, and fix p∗≫0 and w∗>0.

    1. The point (p∗,w∗) lies on H={(p,w):p⋅x(p∗,w∗)=w}, and every (p,w)∈H with p≫0, w>0 satisfies (p,w)≿∗(p∗,w∗).
    2. (Roy’s identity.) If ≿∗ is represented by a differentiable indirect utility function v(p,w), then for every k:
    −∂v/∂w(p∗,w∗)∂v/∂pk​(p∗,w∗)​=xk​(p∗,w∗).
  8. 8

    Rubinstein, Problem Set 6, Problem 1, p. 94

    Step 8: Is v = w/p₁ + w/p₂ a rational consumer’s indirect utility?

    Roy’s identity in action, on an indirect utility taken from a paper: working back from it to a utility function is a skill the last step needs again.

    Read the question

    A paper uses the indirect utility function v(p1​,p2​,w)=w/p1​+w/p2​. Check that it is consistent with the model of the rational consumer.

    • (a) Use Roy’s identity to derive the demand function.
    • (b) Show that if this demand is derived from a smooth utility function, the indifference curve at (x1​,x2​) has slope −x2​​/x1​​.
    • (c) Construct a utility function whose ratio of partial derivatives at (x1​,x2​) is x2​​/x1​​.
    • (d) Compute the indirect utility of that function. Is it the original v? If not, can v be derived from another utility function with the property in (c)?
  9. 9

    Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 87

    Step 9: Hicksian demand and the expenditure function

    Duality begins: the consumer’s problem turned around, from the most satisfaction a budget can buy to the least money a given satisfaction costs.

    Read the definition

    Let ≿ be a preference relation on X=R+K​, p a price vector and z∈X a target bundle. The dual problem D(p,z) is

    xmin​{p⋅x:x≿z}.

    When it has a unique solution, that solution is the Hicksian demand h(p,z), and e(p,z)=p⋅h(p,z) is the expenditure function.

  10. 10

    Rubinstein, Lecture 6, pp. 87–88

    Step 10: Properties of Hicksian demand and the expenditure function

    The dual problem has its own toolkit, including a law of demand with no income effects and the counterpart of Roy’s identity.

    Read the claim

    Let ≿ be a preference relation on X=R+K​. All prices are strictly positive, and each statement assumes that the dual problems it mentions have unique solutions.

    1. h(λp,z)=h(p,z) and e(λp,z)=λe(p,z) for every λ>0.
    2. e(p,z)≤p⋅h(p′,z) for every p′. Hence (p′−p)⋅(h(p′,z)−h(p,z))≤0; raising only the price of good k weakly lowers hk​; and p≤p′ implies e(p,z)≤e(p′,z).
    3. If ≿ is monotone and continuous, then h(p,z)∼z.
    4. If ≿ is monotone and continuous and the dual problem has a unique solution at every (p,z), then h and e are continuous in (p,z).
    5. e is concave in p: e(λp1+(1−λ)p2,z)≥λe(p1,z)+(1−λ)e(p2,z) for λ∈[0,1].
    6. (Dual of Roy’s identity.) The hyperplane {(p,e):e=p⋅h(p∗,z)} passes through (p∗,e(p∗,z)) and lies weakly above the graph of e(⋅,z): p⋅h(p∗,z)≥e(p,z) for all p.
  11. 11

    Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 88

    Step 11: Consumer duality

    This is why the two problems can be used interchangeably: for monotone, continuous preferences they pick out the same bundles.

    Read the claim

    Let ≿ be a monotone and continuous preference relation on X=R+K​, and let p≫0, x∗∈X and w∗>0. Then x∗ solves the prime problem P(p,w∗) if and only if w∗ solves the dual problem D(p,x∗): x∗ is a cheapest bundle among those at least as good as x∗, and p⋅x∗=w∗.

  12. 12

    MIT 14.121, Problem Set 4, Question 1

    Step 12: Recovering utility from the expenditure function

    The destination runs duality backwards, recovering the utility function from the expenditure function, and arrives at the same utility as the worked problem on Roy’s identity.

    Read the question

    A consumer has a continuous, strictly increasing, quasi-concave utility function, and the consumer’s expenditure function is known to be

    e(p1​,p2​,u)=p1​+p2​p1​p2​​u.
    • (a) Show that by considering what happens when one price goes to infinity it is easy to find u(x1​,0) and u(0,x2​).
    • (b) For what values of x1​ does there exist a nonnegative x2​ such that u(x1​,x2​)=u0​?
    • (c) Let x1​ be in the range identified in part (b). By fixing p1​=1 and considering what happens when the consumer faces prices (1,p2​) and has wealth e(1,p2​,u0​), one can find a set of values of x2​ for which u(x1​,x2​)≤u0​. What do you know about the largest x2​ in this set? Use this approach to describe the indifference curve u(x1​,x2​)=u0​, that is, find the function x2​(x1​,u0​) with u(x1​,x2​(x1​,u0​))=u0​.
    • (d) Can you see how to use this information to very quickly find u(x1​,x2​)?
