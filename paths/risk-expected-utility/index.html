Decisions under risk: from lotteries to expected utility and Arrow–Pratt

Independence, the expected utility theorem, and risk attitudes read off the utility of money

Begin with step 1 → First-year PhDAbout 80 minutes

The route

  1. Monotonicity lemma for the independence axiom
  2. Expected utility theorem
  3. Mean–variance preferences and expected utility
  4. Betweenness and the Allais paradox
  5. First-order stochastic dominance
  6. Risk aversion and concavity
  7. How much insurance to buy
  8. Second-order stochastic dominance
  9. Arrow–Pratt
  10. Constant absolute risk aversion
  11. Who pays more for information?

The guide has two halves. The first establishes the model and tests it against mean–variance rankings and the Allais paradox; the second takes prizes to be sums of money, compares lotteries, decision makers and their attitudes to risk, and ends with a question whose answer can go either way.

  1. 1

    Rubinstein, Lecture 7, p. 102

    Step 1: Monotonicity lemma for the independence axiom

    The guide starts with independence, the axiom that sets expected utility apart, and with a first consequence that the main theorem’s proof relies on.

    Read the lemma

    Let Z be a finite set of prizes and ≿ a preference relation on the set L(Z) of lotteries over Z that satisfies the independence axiom. If x,y∈Z and [x]≻[y], then for all 1≥α>β≥0,

    αx⊕(1−α)y≻βx⊕(1−β)y.
  2. 2

    Rubinstein, Lecture 7, p. 104

    Step 2: Expected utility theorem

    The central result. Its geometry is the one to keep in mind for the next steps: indifference lines in the triangle of lotteries are straight and parallel.

    Read the theorem

    Let Z be a finite set of prizes and ≿ a preference relation on L(Z) that satisfies the independence axiom and the continuity axiom. Then there are numbers (v(z))z∈Z​ such that the expected value

    U(p)=z∈Z∑​p(z)v(z)

    represents ≿: for all p,q∈L(Z), p≿q if and only if U(p)≥U(q).

    Try it
  3. 3

    Rubinstein, Problem Set 7, Problem 2, p. 110

    Step 3: Mean–variance preferences and expected utility

    A first test of the model against a popular alternative: rankings by mean and variance, only some of which are expected utilities.

    Read the question

    Based on Markowitz (1959). Preferences over lotteries with monetary prizes can be built from two numbers, the expectation Ex(L) and the variance var(L); such a construction may or may not be consistent with the vNM assumptions.

    • (a) Show that u(L)=Ex(L)−var(L)/4 induces a preference relation that is not consistent with the vNM assumptions. (Consider mixing each of the lotteries [1] and 0.5[0]⊕0.5[4] with 0.5[0]⊕0.5[2].)
    • (b) Show that u(L)=Ex(L)−(Ex(L))2−var(L) is consistent with the vNM assumptions.
  4. 4

    MIT 14.121, Problem Set 2, Question 3

    Step 4: Betweenness and the Allais paradox

    A second test, against the Allais paradox: how much of the triangle’s geometry survives once independence is weakened?

    Read the question

    (MWG Exercise 6.B.5.) There are three possible outcomes. The betweenness axiom (Dekel, 1986) requires that for all lotteries L,L′ and λ∈(0,1), L∼L′ implies λL+(1−λ)L′∼L.

    • (a) Show that a preference relation on lotteries satisfying the independence axiom also satisfies betweenness.
    • (b) Using the simplex representation, show that if the continuity and betweenness axioms hold, indifference curves are straight lines; conversely, show that straight indifference curves imply betweenness. Are these straight lines parallel?
    • (c) Using (b), show that betweenness is weaker (less restrictive) than independence.
    • (d) Show that the choices of the Allais paradox are compatible with betweenness, by exhibiting an indifference map that satisfies it and yields those choices.
  5. 5

    Rubinstein, Lecture 8, p. 114

    Step 5: First-order stochastic dominance

    From here on prizes are sums of money, and the first question is when every expected-utility maximizer who prefers more money agrees on a ranking.

    Read the claim

    Let Z⊆R be a set of monetary prizes and let p,q be lotteries on Z with finite support. Write pD1​q if ∑z​p(z)u(z)≥∑z​q(z)u(z) for every u that is strictly increasing on Z, and let G(p,x)=∑z≥x​p(z) and F(p,x)=∑z≤x​p(z). Then the following are equivalent:

    1. pD1​q;
    2. G(p,x)≥G(q,x) for every real x;
    3. F(p,x)≤F(q,x) for every real x.
  6. 6

    Rubinstein, Lecture 8, p. 116

    Step 6: Risk aversion and concavity

    Next, attitudes to risk. When prizes form an interval, preferring a lottery’s mean for sure is the same as having a utility that bends downward.

    Read the claim

    Let Z⊆R be convex (an interval), and let ≿ be a preference on L(Z) represented by the vNM utility function u. Then ≿ is risk averse, that is [E(p)]≿p for every lottery p with E(p)∈Z, if and only if u is concave on Z.

    Try it
  7. 7

    MIT 14.121, Problem Set 2, Question 1

    Step 7: How much insurance to buy

    Risk aversion put to work in an insurance market, the classic application of the previous step.

    Read the question

    A consumer satisfies the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms and is strictly risk averse. Her wealth is w; with probability p she loses L. She can buy insurance that pays q dollars if the loss occurs, at a cost of πq, where π is the premium per dollar of coverage.

    • (a) How much insurance will she buy, that is, what q (as a function of w,p,L,π) maximizes her expected utility? An equation that implicitly defines q is enough.
    • (b) How much will she buy when insurance is actuarially fair, π=p? What if π>p? Discuss.
  8. 8

    Rubinstein, Problem Set 8, Problem 2, p. 125

    Step 8: Second-order stochastic dominance

    Back to rankings that all decision makers agree on, now only those who are risk averse: a smaller group, so more lotteries can be ranked.

    Read the question

    Based on Rothschild and Stiglitz (1970). Say that p second-order stochastically dominates q, written pD2​q, if p≿q for every preference ≿ satisfying the vNM assumptions, monotonicity and risk aversion.

    • (a) Explain why pD1​q implies pD2​q.
    • (b) For lotteries p and ε, let p+ε be the lottery that yields the prize t with probability ∑α+β=t​p(α)ε(β). Interpret p+ε, and show that if ε has expectation 0, then pD2​(p+ε) for all p.
    • (c) (More difficult) Show that pD2​q if and only if ∑k=0t​[G(p,xk+1​)−G(q,xk+1​)][xk+1​−xk​]≥0 for all t<K, where x0​<⋯<xK​ are all the prizes in the support of p or q, and G(p,x)=∑z≥x​p(z).
  9. 9

    Rubinstein, Lecture 8, p. 118

    Step 9: Arrow–Pratt

    From whether an agent is risk averse to how much: three natural ways of saying that one agent is more risk averse than another, which agree when prizes form an interval.

    Read the claim

    Let Z⊆R be convex, and let ≿1​ and ≿2​ be preferences on L(Z) represented by the expected utilities of u1​ and u2​ respectively, where u1​ and u2​ are strictly increasing and continuous on Z. Then the following are equivalent:

    1. for every lottery p and prize c, p≿1​[c] implies p≿2​[c];
    2. for every lottery p, every certainty equivalent of p under ≿1​ is at most every certainty equivalent of p under ≿2​;
    3. there is a function ϕ, concave on u2​(Z), with u1​(t)=ϕ(u2​(t)) for all t∈Z.
  10. 10

    Rubinstein, Lecture 8, p. 122

    Step 10: Constant absolute risk aversion

    Some attitudes to risk do not depend on how rich the agent is; when prizes can be any real number, this step shows that, for monotone, risk-averse preferences, such attitudes force linear or exponential utility.

    Read the claim

    Let the prize space be the whole real line R, and let the expected utility of u represent a preference ≿ on the lotteries on R with finite support that is monotonic in money (a>b implies [a]≻[b]), risk averse ([E(p)]≿p for every p), and invariant to wealth (w+p≿w+q if and only if w′+p≿w′+q, for all w,w′). Then either u(x)=a+bx for all x, or u(x)=a−be−αx for all x, for some α>0.

  11. 11

    Rubinstein, Problem Set 8, Problem 8, p. 127

    Step 11: Who pays more for information?

    The guide ends on a puzzle built from the comparison of risk aversion above, where intuition points one way and expected utility allows both.

    Read the question

    An agent chooses between actions x and y. Each yields a certain monetary prize that depends on the state of nature, which is A with probability α and B with probability 1−α. The agent maximizes expected utility. Without knowing the state he would choose x; knowing it, he would choose x in state A and y in state B. Discuss the statement: if agent 1 is more risk averse than agent 2, then he is ready to pay more to know the state of nature. Under the expected-utility model, is the statement always true (prove it), always false (prove it), or could it go either way (explain)?
