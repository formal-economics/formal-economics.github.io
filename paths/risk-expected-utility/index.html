Decisions under risk: from lotteries to expected utility and Arrow–Pratt

Independence, the expected utility theorem, and risk attitudes read off the utility of money

On a finite set of prizes, a preference over lotteries that satisfies independence and continuity has an expected-utility representation. When prizes are sums of money, the path reads attitudes toward risk off the utility function: stochastic dominance, risk aversion, comparisons of risk aversion and constant absolute risk aversion, with worked problems on mean–variance rankings, the Allais paradox, insurance and the value of information.

  1. 1

    Lemma · Rubinstein, Lecture 7, p. 102

    Monotonicity lemma for the independence axiom

    Independence: mixing two lotteries with a common third preserves their ranking. A first consequence, on a finite set of prizes: if [x]≻[y], shifting probability from y to x in a lottery over these two prizes is strictly better.

    Lemma

    Let Z be a finite set of prizes and ≿ a preference relation on the set L(Z) of lotteries over Z that satisfies the independence axiom. If x,y∈Z and [x]≻[y], then for all 1≥α>β≥0,

    αx⊕(1−α)y≻βx⊕(1−β)y.

  2. 2

    Theorem · Rubinstein, Lecture 7, p. 104

    Expected utility theorem

    The expected utility theorem (von Neumann–Morgenstern): on a finite set of prizes, a preference over lotteries that satisfies independence and continuity is represented by U(p)=∑z​p(z)v(z) for some numbers v(z). The previous step is one of the lemmas behind it.

    Theorem

    Let Z be a finite set of prizes and ≿ a preference relation on L(Z) that satisfies the independence axiom and the continuity axiom. Then there are numbers (v(z))z∈Z​ such that the expected value

    U(p)=z∈Z∑​p(z)v(z)

    represents ≿: for all p,q∈L(Z), p≿q if and only if U(p)≥U(q).

  3. 3

    Problem · Rubinstein, Problem Set 7, Problem 2, p. 110

    Mean–variance preferences and expected utility

    Mean–variance rankings against the axioms: ranking lotteries by Ex(L)−var(L)/4 can violate the vNM assumptions, while Ex(L)−Ex(L)2−var(L) satisfies them, being the expected value of x−x2.

    The question

    Based on Markowitz (1959). Preferences over lotteries with monetary prizes can be built from two numbers, the expectation Ex(L) and the variance var(L); such a construction may or may not be consistent with the vNM assumptions.

    Betweenness and the Allais paradox

    Betweenness, a weakening of independence, for lotteries over three outcomes: it follows from independence, gives straight but not necessarily parallel indifference lines under continuity, and is compatible with the two choices of the Allais paradox.

    Claim · Rubinstein, Lecture 8, p. 114

    First-order stochastic dominance

    With money prizes, p is ranked at least as high as q by every expected-utility maximizer with a strictly increasing utility exactly when F(p,x)≤F(q,x) for every x: first-order stochastic dominance.

    Risk aversion and concavity

    Risk aversion, [E(p)]≿p, holds exactly when the vNM utility is concave, provided the prizes form an interval. As printed, the claim is stated for every set of prizes; with integer prizes it fails, and the machine-checked version assumes an interval.

    Problem · MIT 14.121, Problem Set 2, Question 1

    How much insurance to buy

    Insurance: a strictly risk-averse consumer insures fully when insurance is actuarially fair, unless the loss is impossible or certain; when it is loaded, she buys at most full coverage as long as her utility is still higher at some wealth above the fully insured level.

    Problem · Rubinstein, Problem Set 8, Problem 2, p. 125

    Second-order stochastic dominance

    Second-order stochastic dominance, the unanimous ranking of monotone, risk-averse expected-utility maximizers (Rothschild and Stiglitz): first-order dominance implies it, adding zero-mean noise yields a dominated lottery, and a partial-sum test characterizes it.

  9. 9

    Claim · Rubinstein, Lecture 8, p. 118

    Arrow–Pratt

    Arrow–Pratt: for strictly increasing, continuous utilities on an interval of prizes, three definitions of “agent 1 is more risk averse than agent 2” agree: whenever 1 weakly prefers a lottery to a sure prize, so does 2; 1’s certainty equivalents are never above 2’s; and u1​=ϕ∘u2​ with ϕ concave. As printed, the claim covers every set of prizes; on the prizes 0,1,2,… the first two definitions come apart.

    Claim · Rubinstein, Lecture 8, p. 122

    Constant absolute risk aversion

    Constant absolute risk aversion: when every real number is a possible prize, an expected-utility preference that is monotone in money, risk averse and invariant to wealth has a utility a+bx or a−be−αx with α>0. As printed, the claim covers every set of prizes, and on a sparse set of prizes it fails.
