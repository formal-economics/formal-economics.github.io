Is it rational? Worked practice from the problem sets to a qualifying exam

Choice procedures, demand patterns and aggregate demand, each tested against the maximization of a preference

Begin with step 1 →

The route

  1. Satisficing is rationalizable
  2. Is the procedure rational?
  3. Choosing from the most common class
  4. Threshold procedures (Kalai, Rubinstein and Spiegler)
  5. Lexicographically rational choice (Rubinstein and Salant)
  6. Choice by rationales (Cherepanov, Feddersen and Sandroni)
  7. Luce’s choice axiom
  8. Which behaviour patterns are rationalizable?
  9. Giffen goods are inferior
  10. An economy with no positive representative consumer
  11. Demand systems and expected utility

Most steps ask the same question of a different behaviour, which makes the answers easy to compare: what separates the behaviours that pass from those that fail? It helps to have read the guides to rational choice and the consumer first.

  1. 1

    Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 30

    Step 1: Satisficing is rationalizable

    A warm-up with a twist: Simon’s satisficing looks nothing like maximization, and the first lesson is that appearances do not settle the question.

    Read the claim

    Let (X,D) be a context, v a valuation, v∗ a threshold and O an ordering of X such that every A∈D has a unique satisficing choice. Then Cv,v∗,O​ can be rationalized: there is a preference relation ≿ on X such that every A∈D has a unique best element under ≿ and Cv,v∗,O​(A)=C≿​(A) for all A∈D.

    Try it
  2. 2

    Rubinstein, Problem Set 3, Problem 1, p. 44

    Step 2: Is the procedure rational?

    Five procedures, described in words, to sort into rational and not; the lesson is that what is maximized does not matter, while dependence on the menu does.

    Read the question

    For each procedure, discuss whether it can be described in the choice model and whether it is compatible with the rational man paradigm:

    • (a) the decision maker maximizes another person’s suffering;
    • (b) he asks his two children to rank the alternatives and chooses the one with the lowest average score;
    • (c) he has an ideal point in mind and chooses the alternative closest to it according to some distance function;
    • (d) he looks for the alternative that appears most often in the choice set;
    • (e) he has an ordering of X in mind and always chooses the median element.
  3. 3

    Rubinstein, Problem Set 3, Problem 4, p. 45

    Step 3: Choosing from the most common class

    A procedure that behaves perfectly on every pair of alternatives, which shows that consistency on pairs is not enough.

    Read the question

    A decision maker has a strict ordering ≿ of X and assigns to each x a natural number class(x). From a choice problem A he chooses the best element among those belonging to the most common class in A; if several classes are most common, he picks the best element of a most common class with the highest class number.

    • (a) Define xPy if x is chosen from {x,y}. Show that P is a strict ordering (complete, asymmetric and transitive).
    • (b) Is the procedure consistent with the rational man paradigm?
  4. 4

    Rubinstein, Problem Set 3, Problem 5, p. 45

    Step 4: Threshold procedures (Kalai, Rubinstein and Spiegler)

    Two threshold procedures that look almost alike; whether the switch is triggered by the menu as a whole or by one alternative in it decides which is rational.

    Read the question

    Interpret the following procedures and determine whether either is consistent with the rational man paradigm. The primitives are two one-to-one functions u and v on X.

    • (a) With a number v∗: for a choice problem A, let a∗ maximize u and b∗ maximize v over A; choose a∗ if v(a∗)≥v∗ and b∗ if v(a∗)<v∗.
    • (b) With a number u∗: choose the u-maximizer a∗ if u(a∗)≥u∗, and the v-maximizer b∗ if u(a∗)<u∗.
  5. 5

    Rubinstein, Problem Set 3, Problem 7, pp. 46–47

    Step 5: Lexicographically rational choice (Rubinstein and Salant)

    Beyond the yes-or-no question, a characterization: choice guided by the most notable alternative on the menu is pinned down by a single property of choices.

    Read the question

    Let X be finite. A choice function c is lexicographically rational if there are preference relations {≻a​}a∈X​ and an ordering O of X such that c(A) is the ≻a​-maximal element of A, where a is the O-maximal element of A: the decision maker notices the most notable element and then applies its ordering. c satisfies the reference-point property if every A contains some a such that a∈A′′⊂A′⊂A and c(A′)∈A′′ imply c(A′′)=c(A′).

    • (a) Show that c is lexicographically rational iff it satisfies the reference-point property.
    • (b) Find a choice function satisfying the reference-point property that is not stated explicitly in the language of lexicographic rationality.
  6. 6

    Rubinstein, Problem Set 3, Problem 8, p. 47

    Step 6: Choice by rationales (Cherepanov, Feddersen and Sandroni)

    Another characterization, for a decision maker who first rules out what she cannot justify and then chooses; a weaker form of the weak axiom captures it exactly.

    Read the question

    A decision maker on a finite set X has in mind an asymmetric, complete relation ≻ (not necessarily transitive) and rationales ≻1​,…,≻K​, each asymmetric and transitive; from A he chooses the ≻-best alternative among those maximal in A for at least one rationale (an alternative x is maximal in A for a relation if it is ranked above every other y∈A). A choice function satisfies WWARP if, for {x,y}⊂B1​⊂B2​ with x=y and c{x,y}=c(B2​)=x, we have c(B1​)=y.

    • (a) Show that a choice function satisfies WWARP iff it is rationalized in this sense (construct one rationale for each choice problem).

    In the warm-glow procedure the decision maker has a moral ordering ≿M​ and a selfish ordering ≿S​, and chooses the most moral alternative m unless he loses too much relative to the most selfish one s: he chooses m if m≿S​l(s) and s otherwise, where s≿S​l(s) and s≿S​s′ implies l(s)≿S​l(s′).

    • (b) Show that this procedure satisfies WWARP.
    • (c) Show directly that it is rationalized.
  7. 7

    Rubinstein, Problem Set 3, Problem 9, p. 47

    Step 7: Luce’s choice axiom

    Choice becomes random: a consistency condition on choice probabilities leads to Luce’s model, in which each alternative carries a fixed weight.

    Read the question

    A probabilistic choice function assigns C(a∣A)>0 to every a∈A⊆X, with ∑a∈A​C(a∣A)=1; for B⊂A let C(B∣A)=∑a∈B​C(a∣A). It satisfies path independence (PI) if C(a∣A)=C(a∣B)C(B∣A) for any a∈B⊂A.

    • (a) Explain PI and give two examples of reasonable probabilistic choice functions that do not satisfy it.
    • (b) Prove that under PI there is v:X→R+​ with C(a∣A)=v(a)/∑x∈A​v(x).
  8. 8

    Rubinstein, Problem Set 5, Problem 4, p. 79

    Step 8: Which behaviour patterns are rationalizable?

    From menus to budget sets: two simple patterns of demand put to the same test, with opposite answers.

    Read the question

    Determine whether these consumer behaviour patterns are fully rationalizable (K=2):

    • (a) the consumer consumes up to the quantity 1 of commodity 1 and spends his remaining wealth on commodity 2;
    • (b) the consumer chooses the bundle (x1​,x2​) with x1​/x2​=p1​/p2​ that costs w. Does the utility function u(x)=x12​+x22​ rationalize this behaviour?
    Try it
  9. 9

    Rubinstein, Problem Set 5, Problem 7, p. 80

    Step 9: Giffen goods are inferior

    What consistency buys: under the weak axiom, a demand curve that slopes upward forces the good to be inferior somewhere.

    Read the question

    A commodity k is Giffen if its demand increases with pk​, and inferior if its demand decreases with wealth. Show that if at some (p,w) the demand for commodity k increases with its price, then at some (p′,w′) the demand for k falls when w increases: being Giffen implies being inferior.

  10. 10

    MIT 14.121, Problem Set 4, Question 2

    Step 10: An economy with no positive representative consumer

    From one consumer to many: even when each consumer is rational, total demand can fail the test.

    Read the question

    (MWG Exercise 4.D.5.) Suppose that there are I consumers and that consumer i’s utility function is ui​(xi​), with demand function xi​(p,wi​). Consumer i’s wealth is generated by a wealth distribution rule wi​=αi​w, where αi​≥0 and ∑i​αi​=1. Provide an example, that is, a set of utility functions, in which this economy does not admit a positive representative consumer.

  11. 11

    BU Qualifying Exam (2020), Question 1, p. 2

    Step 11: Demand systems and expected utility

    The guide ends with a qualifying-exam question that brings it all together: demand, attitudes to risk, and whether a ranking of lotteries can come from expected utility.

    Read the question

    (a) There are two goods, A and B, with prices pA​,pB​>0 and income M>0.

    • (a.i) Derive the demand functions xA​(pA​,pB​,M) and xB​(pA​,pB​,M) implied by maximizing the Cobb–Douglas utility U(xA​,xB​)=xAα​xB1−α​.
    • (a.ii) A consumer has demand functions xA​=a1​M/pA​+a2​M/pB​ and xB​=b1​M/pA​+b2​M/pB​ for all pA​,pB​,M>0. For what parameter values do these demands satisfy the Slutsky symmetry conditions?
    • (a.iii) For these demand functions, describe conditions on the parameters ai​ and bi​ that are necessary and sufficient for a complete and transitive preference order to give rise to them.

    (b) Consider the von Neumann utility index u(x)=x​ for x≤100 and u(x)=10+x−100​ for x>100. Graph it. Does u imply risk aversion over all lotteries with nonnegative prizes? If yes, prove it; if not, provide a lottery that demonstrates the violation.

    (c) Can an expected utility preference order give rise to the rankings

    (200,181​;100,91​;40,92​;0,1811​)≻(100,31​;40,32​)≻(200,121​;0,1211​)?​

    Provide your reasoning. Here (x1​,p1​;x2​,p2​;…) is the lottery that gives prize xi​ with probability pi​.
