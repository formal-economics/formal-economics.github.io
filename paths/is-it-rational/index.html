A question recurs throughout the problem sets: is a described behaviour consistent with maximizing a single preference? This path works through it for choice procedures, for individual and aggregate demand, and for a qualifying-exam question that asks it of a demand system and of rankings of lotteries. It assumes the rational-choice and consumer paths.

  1. 1

    Step 1 · Claim · Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 30

    Satisficing is rationalizable

    Simon’s satisficing examines alternatives in a fixed order and takes the first whose value reaches a threshold, or the last one if none does. It looks like a departure from maximization, yet every satisficing choice function is rationalizable: choices alone cannot tell the two apart.

    Claim

    Let (X,D) be a context, v a valuation, v∗ a threshold and O an ordering of X such that every A∈D has a unique satisficing choice. Then Cv,v∗,O​ can be rationalized: there is a preference relation ≿ on X such that every A∈D has a unique best element under ≿ and Cv,v∗,O​(A)=C≿​(A) for all A∈D.

    Step 2 · Problem · Rubinstein, Problem Set 3, Problem 1, p. 44

    Is the procedure rational?

    Five procedures put to the test: maximizing any fixed ranking, or closeness to an ideal point under any distance, is rationalizable; choosing the alternatives with the lowest total rank in two children’s rankings of the menu, or the median of a fixed ordering, need not be.

  3. 3

    Step 3 · Problem · Rubinstein, Problem Set 3, Problem 4, p. 45

    Choosing from the most common class

    Choosing the best member of the most common class induces a strict ordering on pairs, yet the procedure need not be rationalizable: consistency on pairs is not enough.

    The question

    A decision maker has a strict ordering ≿ of X and assigns to each x a natural number class(x). From a choice problem A he chooses the best element among those belonging to the most common class in A; if several classes are most common, he picks the best element of a most common class with the highest class number.

    Threshold procedures (Kalai, Rubinstein and Spiegler)

    Two threshold procedures (Kalai, Rubinstein and Spiegler) that switch from maximizing u to maximizing v: switching when the best available u-value falls below a threshold is always rationalizable, while switching on the v-value of the u-best alternative need not be.

    Step 5 · Problem · Rubinstein, Problem Set 3, Problem 7, pp. 46–47

    Lexicographically rational choice (Rubinstein and Salant)

    Lexicographic rationality (Rubinstein and Salant): the decision maker notices the most notable alternative in the menu and maximizes the ordering attached to it. On a finite set these are exactly the choice functions with the reference-point property.

    Choice by rationales (Cherepanov, Feddersen and Sandroni)

  7. 7

    Step 7 · Problem · Rubinstein, Problem Set 3, Problem 9, p. 47

    Luce’s choice axiom

    Stochastic choice (Luce): if positive choice probabilities satisfy C(a∣A)=C(a∣B)C(B∣A) whenever a∈B⊆A, then some weights v(a)>0 give C(a∣A)=v(a)/∑x∈A​v(x). As printed, the normalization of the probabilities also covers the empty menu, where it cannot hold; the machine-checked definition imposes it on nonempty menus.

    Which behaviour patterns are rationalizable?

    The same test for demand: buying up to one unit of good 1 and spending the rest on good 2 is fully rationalizable on its natural reading, while buying quantities in proportion to prices is not, and u=x12​+x22​ does not rationalize it either.

  9. 9

    Step 9 · Problem · Rubinstein, Problem Set 5, Problem 7, p. 80

    Giffen goods are inferior

    Revealed preference has bite: if demand satisfies the weak axiom, a good whose demand rises with its own price somewhere has demand falling with wealth somewhere, so a Giffen good is inferior. As printed, the claim is made for every demand function that is budget-feasible and homogeneous of degree zero, where a counterexample shows it can fail.

    Demand systems and expected utility

    The qualifying exam asks the same question: when the demand system xA​=a1​M/pA​+a2​M/pB​, xB​=b1​M/pA​+b2​M/pB​ is Slutsky symmetric and when a complete, transitive preference gives rise to it; whether a spliced square-root utility index is risk averse; and whether any expected-utility order ranks three given lotteries in a given strict order, which none does, since the first is a mixture of the other two.
