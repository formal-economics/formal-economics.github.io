  1. Question 1MIT 14.121 · Problem Set 1

    Indifference and strict preference

    Let ≿ be a complete, reflexive and transitive preference relation on a set X, and define x∼y⟺(x≿y)∧(y≿x) and x≻y⟺(x≿y)∧¬(y≿x). Prove:

    1. ∼ is symmetric: if x∼y then y∼x;
    2. ∼ is reflexive: x∼x;
    3. ∼ is transitive: if x∼y and y∼z then x∼z, so that, with parts 1 and 2, ∼ is an equivalence relation;
    4. if x≻y and y≿z, then x≻z.

    Answer

    Shown: ∼ is symmetric.

  2. Question 2MIT 14.121 · Problem Set 1

    Utility is ordinal

    (MWG Exercise 1.B.3.) Show that if f:R→R is strictly increasing and u:X→R is a utility function representing the preference relation ≿, then v=f∘u, v(x)=f(u(x)), is also a utility function representing ≿.

    Answer

    Shown, for any binary relation. “Represents” is read here as x≿y⟺u(x)≥u(y), our formulation of the notion the question uses.

  3. Question 3MIT 14.121 · Problem Set 1

    Lexicographic preferences have no utility representation

    With X finite, every complete, reflexive and transitive preference has a utility representation. Now let X=R2 and define (x1​,x2​)≿(y1​,y2​) iff x1​>y1​, or x1​=y1​ and x2​≥y2​: lexicographic preferences.

    1. a

      Show that ≿ is a complete, reflexive and transitive preference relation on X.

    2. b

      Show that no utility function u:X→R represents ≿. What does this mean for the theorem that finite X always admits a representation?

    3. c

      A theorem in Jehle and Reny shows that a complete, reflexive and transitive preference on Rn that is also continuous and strictly monotone has a utility representation. Which of these properties do lexicographic preferences violate?

    Answer

    Shown: the lexicographic relation is a preference relation.

  4. Question 4MIT 14.121 · Problem Set 1

    Do survey answers reveal preferences?

    Not a formal statement

    A study surveys 2034 Australians to place a dollar value on preserving the Kakadu Conservation Zone and finds benefits far exceeding costs. Critics argue that survey answers may not reflect true preferences: respondents have had little time to think, may worry about the interviewer’s opinion, or may enjoy seeing themselves as virtuous. Using the material of the course, how could one assess whether the survey results reflect true preferences? Which preference axioms might the answers violate, how might a survey be designed to detect such violations, and what should be done if they are found?

  5. Question 1MIT 14.121 · Problem Set 2

    How much insurance to buy

    A consumer satisfies the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms and is strictly risk averse. Her wealth is w; with probability p she loses L. She can buy insurance that pays q dollars if the loss occurs, at a cost of πq, where π is the premium per dollar of coverage.

    1. a

      How much insurance will she buy, that is, what q (as a function of w,p,L,π) maximizes her expected utility? An equation that implicitly defines q is enough.

      Answer

      Our answer: the optimal coverage solves the first-order condition p(1−π)u′(w−L+(1−π)q)=π(1−p)u′(w−πq), with supporting slopes in place of derivatives where u has a kink.

    2. b

      How much will she buy when insurance is actuarially fair, π=p? What if π>p? Discuss.

      Answer

      Our answer: full coverage, q=L, whenever the loss is uncertain (0<p<1); if p=0 or p=1, every coverage level is optimal.

    Full solution →

  6. Question 2MIT 14.121 · Problem Set 2

    To feed the parking meter or not

    Corrected

    Citizens share a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility u(w) that is as smooth as needed, strictly increasing and concave. Each must park at a meter costing m; an unpaid meter is spotted with probability p and fined f, and otherwise nothing is paid. Let Δ be the gain from feeding the meter: expected utility from feeding it minus expected utility from parking illegally.

    1. a

      The town can hire more police, raising p, or raise the fine f. Compute the elasticity of Δ with respect to p and compare it with the elasticity with respect to f. To which policy change are citizens more responsive?

      Answer

      Our answer: εp​=p[u(w)−u(w−f)]/Δ, the expected utility cost of the fine relative to the gain from paying.

    2. b

      Citizens differ in initial wealth w0​∈[w​,wˉ]; suppose Δ is decreasing in initial wealth and that some citizens feed the meters while others park illegally. Write a simple expression that determines which group feeds the meters and which parks illegally, and show that both groups being nonempty requires the meter price m to exceed the expected fine pf.

      Answer

      Our answer: a unique threshold w∗ divides the citizens; those poorer than w∗ feed the meter and those richer park illegally.

    3. c

      Qualitatively, how does the set of people who feed the meters change with f, m and p? Prove your answer and interpret it.

      Answer

      Our answer: the set of citizens who feed the meter grows weakly as the fine rises.

  8. Question 3MIT 14.121 · Problem Set 3

    Quasi-linear preferences have no wealth effects

    Let the consumption set be (−∞,∞)×R+n−1​, and suppose that preferences are strictly convex and quasi-linear in the first good. Normalize p1​=1.

    1. a

      Show that the Marshallian demand functions for goods 2,…,n are independent of wealth.

    2. b

      Show that the Hicksian demand functions for goods 2,…,n are independent of the target utility.

    3. c

      Argue that the indirect utility function can be written in the form v(p,w)=w+φ(p) for some function φ. What is the form of the expenditure function?

    Answer

    Shown: the Marshallian demand for goods 2,…,n is independent of wealth.

  9. Question 4MIT 14.121 · Problem Set 3

    Demand for the utility max{ax, ay} + min{x, y}

    A consumer of two goods has utility function u(x,y)=max{ax,ay}+min{x,y}, with 0<a<1.

    1. a

      Draw the indifference curves for these preferences.

    2. b

      Derive the Marshallian and Hicksian demands.

    Answer

    Our answer: the indifference curve at level c consists of two straight segments meeting on the diagonal at (c/(1+a),c/(1+a)), with slope −a below the diagonal, down to (c/a,0), and slope −1/a above it, up to (0,c/a).
