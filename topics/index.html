I

Part I: Individual choice

Preferences

Preference relations are the primitive of choice theory: complete and transitive rankings, from which strict preference and indifference are derived. The two classical definitions, by questionnaire and by relation, describe the same objects.

Open the field

Famous results

  • Equivalence of the two definitions of preferences
  • PI-transitivity: strict preference, then indifference

Utility representation

A utility function represents a preference when higher numbers mean better alternatives. Preferences on countable sets always have one, continuous preferences on connected subsets of Euclidean space have a continuous one (Debreu), and the lexicographic preference on the square has none.

Open the field

Famous results

  • Debreu’s theorem
  • The lexicographic counterexample
  • Utility on countable sets
  • Utility is ordinal

Choice and rationalizability

Choice functions record what a decision maker picks from each set of alternatives. The central question is rationalizability, whether the choices maximize some preference; conditions α and β and the weak axiom of revealed preference answer it.

Open the field

Famous results

  • The weak axiom (WARP)
  • Rationalizability by α and β
  • Satisficing is rationalizable
  • Rationalizability of choice functions satisfying condition α
II

Part II: Consumers and producers

Consumer preferences

Preferences over bundles carry economic structure: monotonicity, continuity, convexity, homotheticity, quasi-linearity and differentiability. Each has a counterpart in utility, such as quasi-concavity for convexity and homogeneity for homotheticity.

Open the field

Famous results

  • Convexity and quasi-concavity
  • Homothetic utility
  • Quasi-linear utility
  • Utility representation of monotone continuous preferences

Consumer demand and duality

The consumer chooses a best bundle in the budget set, and demand records that choice at every price and wealth. The entries run from Walras’ law and first-order conditions to Roy’s identity and the duality with expenditure minimization.

Open the field

Famous results

  • Roy’s identity
  • Walras’ law
  • Slutsky’s compensated law of demand
  • Consumer duality

Producers and the firm

A producer is described by a technology, the set of feasible plans, or by a production function. Profit maximization gives the profit and supply functions, with Hotelling’s lemma, and cost minimization gives the cost function.

Open the field

Famous results

  • Hotelling’s lemma
  • Properties of the supply function
  • The technology derived from a production function
III

Part III: Uncertainty and risk

Choice under uncertainty

Choice under uncertainty ranks lotteries over prizes. Preferences satisfying independence and continuity have an expected-utility representation (von Neumann–Morgenstern), and de Finetti’s theorem derives subjective probabilities from preferences over bets.

Open the field

Famous results

  • Expected utility theorem
  • De Finetti’s theorem
  • Monotonicity lemma for the independence axiom

Risk attitudes

For monetary lotteries, attitudes toward risk are read off the vNM utility. On an interval of prizes, risk aversion is concavity and being more risk averse is a concave transformation; first-order stochastic dominance is the ranking that every expected-utility maximizer who prefers more money shares.

Open the field

Famous results

  • Arrow–Pratt
  • First-order stochastic dominance
  • Risk aversion and concavity
  • Constant absolute risk aversion
IV

Part IV: Behavioral decision theory

Behavioral decision theory

Some choice procedures depart from maximizing a single preference, or reach consistent choices by other routes. Examples include Simon’s satisficing, choice with a default and a bias toward it, choice from lists and Luce’s probabilistic choice.

Open the field

Famous results

  • Satisficing is rationalizable
  • Characterization of the default-bias procedure
V

Part V: Collective choice

Social choice

Social choice aggregates individual preferences into a social ranking or decision. With at least three alternatives, Arrow’s theorem leaves only dictatorships under Pareto and independence of irrelevant alternatives; related entries treat strategy-proofness, majority rule and single-peaked preferences.

Open the field

Famous results

  • Arrow’s impossibility theorem
  • Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem
