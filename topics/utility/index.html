Fields of economics/Part I · Individual choice

Utility representation

A utility function represents a preference when higher numbers mean better alternatives. Preferences on countable sets always have one, continuous preferences on connected subsets of Euclidean space have a continuous one (Debreu), and the lexicographic preference on the square has none.

Results to know

The landmarks of the field, to read first.

  1. x≿y⟺U(x)≥U(y) Proposition Debreu’s theorem A continuous preference relation on a convex, or more generally connected, subset of Euclidean space has a continuous utility function. Lecture 2, p. 18
  2. ∄U: x≿L​y⟺U(x)≥U(y) Claim The lexicographic counterexample The lexicographic preference on the unit square is complete and transitive, yet no real-valued function represents it. Lecture 2, pp. 15–16
  3. X countable⇒∃U:X→(0,1) Claim Utility on countable sets Every preference relation on a countable set has a utility representation with values in (0, 1). Lecture 2, pp. 14–15
  4. U represents ≿⇒f∘U represents ≿ Claim Utility is ordinal Composing a utility function with any strictly increasing function gives another representation of the same preference. Lecture 2, p. 13

Definitions

  1. Utility representation A utility function represents a preference relation when one alternative is at least as good as another exactly when its utility is at least as high. Lecture 2, p. 12
  2. Lexicographic preferences A list of preferences, consulted in order of priority, induces a lexicographic preference. Lecture 2, p. 15
  3. The lexicographic preference on the unit square The standard example: on [0, 1]², the first coordinate decides and the second breaks ties. Lecture 2, p. 15
  4. Continuity of preferences (open-ball form) Strict preference is preserved under small perturbations of both alternatives. Lecture 2, p. 16
  5. Continuity of preferences (closed-graph form) The graph of a continuous preference relation is a closed set. Lecture 2, p. 17

More results

  1. Utility representation on finite sets Every preference relation on a finite set has a utility representation with natural-number values. Claim Lecture 2, p. 14
  2. Lexicographic preferences are preference relations The lexicographic preference induced by finitely many preference relations is complete and transitive. Lemma Lecture 2, p. 15
  3. Equivalence of the two definitions of continuity On a metric space, the open-ball and closed-graph definitions of continuous preferences agree. Claim Lecture 2, p. 17

Also relevant, from other fields

  1. Best and worst elements of finite sets Every nonempty finite set has a best and a worst element under a preference relation. Lemma Lecture 2, pp. 13–14
  2. Utility representation of monotone continuous preferences Monotone and continuous preferences over bundles have a continuous utility representation. Claim Lecture 4, p. 50
  3. Convexity and quasi-concavity A represented preference is convex exactly when its utility function is quasi-concave. Claim Lecture 4, p. 53
  4. Convex preferences need not have a concave utility Corrected Some continuous and convex preference on ℝ is represented by no concave function. Claim Lecture 4, p. 54
  5. Homothetic utility Homothetic, continuous and monotone preferences have a continuous utility that is homogeneous of degree one. Claim Lecture 4, p. 57
  6. Quasi-linear utility Monotone, continuous preferences that are quasi-linear and strongly monotone in good 1 have a utility equal to the amount of good 1 plus a function of the other goods. Claim Lecture 4, p. 58
  7. Linear utility from quasi-linearity in all goods Continuous, strongly monotone preferences that are quasi-linear in every good have a positive linear utility. Claim Lecture 4, p. 60
  8. Recovering utility from the expenditure function Corrected From e(p, u) = p₁p₂u/(p₁ + p₂) one recovers utility on the axes, each indifference curve x₂ = (√u₀ − √x₁)², and finally u = (√x₁ + √x₂)². Problem MIT 14.121, Problem Set 4, Question 1
