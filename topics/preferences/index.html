Preferences

Preference relations are the primitive of choice theory: complete and transitive rankings, from which strict preference and indifference are derived. The two classical definitions, by questionnaire and by relation, describe the same objects.

2 definitions4 results7 problemsJEL D01, D11

The results to know 3

  1. Preference relation The standard definition: a reflexive, complete and transitive binary relation “at least as good as”. Rubinstein, Lecture 1, p. 6
  2. Equivalence of the two definitions of preferences Questionnaire preferences and preference relations are in one-to-one correspondence, with matching meanings of strict preference and indifference. Rubinstein, Lecture 1, pp. 7–9
  3. PI-transitivity: strict preference, then indifference From the questionnaire definition: x ≻ y and y ∼ z imply x ≻ z. Rubinstein, Lecture 1, p. 5

Proved in the problem sets Szpilrajn’s extension theorem · Dushnik–Miller theorem · Kannai–Peleg theorem · Kreps’s definition of preference

Definitions 2

  1. Preferences as consistent answers to a questionnaire Rubinstein’s primary definition: preferences are a function answering every pairwise comparison, free of order effects and transitive. Rubinstein, Lecture 1, p. 3
  2. Preference relation The standard definition: a reflexive, complete and transitive binary relation “at least as good as”. Rubinstein, Lecture 1, p. 6

Results 4

  1. PI-transitivity: strict preference, then indifference From the questionnaire definition: x ≻ y and y ∼ z imply x ≻ z. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 1, p. 5
  2. IP-transitivity: indifference, then strict preference From the questionnaire definition: x ∼ y and y ≻ z imply x ≻ z. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 1, p. 5
  3. Equivalence of the two definitions of preferences Questionnaire preferences and preference relations are in one-to-one correspondence, with matching meanings of strict preference and indifference. Theorem Rubinstein, Lecture 1, pp. 7–9
  4. Best and worst elements of finite sets Every nonempty finite set has a best and a worst element under a preference relation. Lemma Rubinstein, Lecture 2, pp. 13–14

Problems 7

In the practice room
  1. Problem 1. Indifference classes form a partition The indifference classes of a preference relation split the set of alternatives into disjoint blocks. Problem Set 1 Easy
  2. Problem 2. Kreps’s definition of preference · Kreps's definition of preferences Taking strict preference as the primitive, with asymmetry and negative transitivity, is equivalent to the traditional definition. Problem Set 1 Standard
  3. Problem 3. Kannai–Peleg theorem · Ranking menus (Kannai and Peleg) Two natural axioms for ranking menus are independent and can hold together, but they clash as soon as three alternatives are strictly ranked. Problem Set 1 Difficult 4 parts
  4. Problem 4. Szpilrajn’s extension theorem · Extending an acyclic relation to an ordering On a finite set, an asymmetric relation without cycles can always be completed to a strict linear ordering. Problem Set 1 Moderately difficult
  5. Problem 5. Dushnik–Miller theorem · Multiple criteria and incomplete preferences Comparing alternatives by unanimity over several complete rankings yields exactly the asymmetric, transitive relations on a finite set. Problem Set 1 Difficult 3 parts
  6. Problem 6. The Shepard scale An auditory illusion of an endlessly rising scale, offered as an analogy for intransitive comparisons. Problem Set 1 Fun Informal
  7. Question 1. Indifference and strict preference For a complete, reflexive and transitive preference, indifference is an equivalence relation, and strict preference followed by weak preference is strict. MIT 14.121 · Problem Set 1

Also relevant, from other topics 6

  1. Continuity of preferences (open-ball form) Strict preference is preserved under small perturbations of both alternatives. Definition Rubinstein, Lecture 2, p. 16
  2. Continuity of preferences (closed-graph form) The graph of a continuous preference relation is a closed set. Definition Rubinstein, Lecture 2, p. 17
  3. Equivalence of the two definitions of continuity On a metric space, the open-ball and closed-graph definitions of continuous preferences agree. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 2, p. 17
  4. Adam’s apples Four mild-looking conditions on preferences over apple streams imply that Adam would give up two apples a day from day 4 onward for a single apple today. Problem Rubinstein, Problem Set 8, Problem 5, p. 126
  5. Lexicographic preferences have no utility representation Lexicographic preferences on ℝ² are complete, reflexive and transitive, yet no utility function represents them; of the standard axioms they violate only continuity. Problem MIT 14.121, Problem Set 1, Question 3
  6. Do survey answers reveal preferences? A discussion question on contingent valuation: which preference axioms might survey answers violate, and how could a survey test for such violations? Problem MIT 14.121, Problem Set 1, Question 4 Informal
