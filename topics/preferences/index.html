Fields of economics/Part I · Individual choice

Preferences

Preference relations are the primitive of choice theory: complete and transitive rankings, from which strict preference and indifference are derived. The two classical definitions, by questionnaire and by relation, describe the same objects.

Results to know

The landmarks of the field, to read first.

  1. Theorem Equivalence of the two definitions of preferences Questionnaire preferences and preference relations are in one-to-one correspondence, with matching meanings of strict preference and indifference. Lecture 1, pp. 7–9
  2. Claim PI-transitivity: strict preference, then indifference From the questionnaire definition: x ≻ y and y ∼ z imply x ≻ z. Lecture 1, p. 5

Definitions

  1. Preferences as consistent answers to a questionnaire Rubinstein’s primary definition: preferences are a function answering every pairwise comparison, free of order effects and transitive. Lecture 1, p. 3
  2. Preference relation The standard definition: a reflexive, complete and transitive binary relation “at least as good as”. Lecture 1, p. 6

More results

  1. IP-transitivity: indifference, then strict preference From the questionnaire definition: x ∼ y and y ≻ z imply x ≻ z. Claim Lecture 1, p. 5
  2. Best and worst elements of finite sets Every nonempty finite set has a best and a worst element under a preference relation. Lemma Lecture 2, pp. 13–14

Lecture Notes in Microeconomic Theory

  1. Problem 1. Indifference classes form a partition The indifference classes of a preference relation split the set of alternatives into disjoint blocks. Problem Set 1 Easy
  2. Problem 2. Kreps’s definition of preference Taking strict preference as the primitive, with asymmetry and negative transitivity, is equivalent to the traditional definition. Problem Set 1 Standard
  3. Problem 3. Kannai–Peleg theorem · Ranking menus (Kannai and Peleg) Two natural axioms for ranking menus are independent and can hold together, but they clash as soon as three alternatives are strictly ranked. Problem Set 1 Difficult
  4. Problem 4. Szpilrajn’s extension theorem · Extending an acyclic relation to an ordering On a finite set, an asymmetric relation without cycles can always be completed to a strict linear ordering. Problem Set 1 Moderately difficult
  5. Problem 5. Dushnik–Miller theorem · Multiple criteria and incomplete preferences Comparing alternatives by unanimity over several complete rankings yields exactly the asymmetric, transitive relations on a finite set. Problem Set 1 Difficult
  6. Problem 6. The Shepard scale Informal An auditory illusion of an endlessly rising scale, offered as an analogy for intransitive comparisons. Problem Set 1 Fun

MIT 14.121 Microeconomic Theory I

  1. Question 1. Indifference and strict preference For a complete, reflexive and transitive preference, indifference is an equivalence relation, and strict preference followed by weak preference is strict. Problem Set 1

Also relevant, from other fields

  1. Continuity of preferences (open-ball form) Strict preference is preserved under small perturbations of both alternatives. Definition Lecture 2, p. 16
  2. Continuity of preferences (closed-graph form) The graph of a continuous preference relation is a closed set. Definition Lecture 2, p. 17
  3. Equivalence of the two definitions of continuity On a metric space, the open-ball and closed-graph definitions of continuous preferences agree. Claim Lecture 2, p. 17
  4. Adam’s apples Four mild-looking conditions on preferences over apple streams imply that Adam would give up two apples a day from day 4 onward for a single apple today. Problem Problem Set 8, Problem 5, p. 126
  5. Lexicographic preferences have no utility representation Lexicographic preferences on ℝ² are complete, reflexive and transitive, yet no utility function represents them; of the standard axioms they violate only continuity. Problem MIT 14.121, Problem Set 1, Question 3
  6. Do survey answers reveal preferences? Informal A discussion question on contingent valuation: which preference axioms might survey answers violate, and how could a survey test for such violations? Problem MIT 14.121, Problem Set 1, Question 4
