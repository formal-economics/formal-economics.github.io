Topics/Part II · Consumers and producers

Consumer preferences

Preferences over bundles carry economic structure: monotonicity, continuity, convexity, homotheticity, quasi-linearity and differentiability. Each has a counterpart in utility, such as quasi-concavity for convexity and homogeneity for homotheticity.

The results to know 4

  1. Convexity and quasi-concavity A represented preference is convex exactly when its utility function is quasi-concave. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 53
  2. Homothetic utility Homothetic, continuous and monotone preferences have a continuous utility that is homogeneous of degree one. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 57
  3. Quasi-linear utility u(x)=x1​+v(x2​,…,xK​) Monotone, continuous preferences that are quasi-linear and strongly monotone in good 1 have a utility equal to the amount of good 1 plus a function of the other goods. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 58
  4. Utility representation of monotone continuous preferences Monotone and continuous preferences over bundles have a continuous utility representation. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 50

Definitions 20

  1. Monotone preferences More of every good is at least as good; strictly more of every good is strictly better. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 49
  2. Strongly monotone preferences Any increase in any good, keeping the others, is strictly better. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 49
  3. Nonsatiation Arbitrarily close to a bundle there is a strictly better one. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 49
  4. Continuity of consumer preferences Strict preferences between bundles survive small changes in either bundle. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 49
  5. Convex preferences: first definition Moving toward a weakly better bundle never makes things worse. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 52
  6. Convex preferences: second definition A mixture of two bundles is at least as good as one of them. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 52
  7. Convex preferences: third definition Upper contour sets are convex. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 52
  8. Strictly convex preferences Strict mixtures of distinct bundles that are at least as good as y are strictly better than y. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 53
  9. Concave and quasi-concave functions A reminder of the mathematical definitions used in the link between convexity and utility. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 53 Informal
  10. Λ-convex preferences Convexity as the existence, at every alternative, of a criterion that must improve. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 54
  11. Λ-strictly convex preferences The strict version of Λ-convexity, covering moves to equally good alternatives. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 54
  12. Single-peaked preferences Preferences on an interval with a unique ideal point, declining on both sides. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 55
  13. Algebraic linear orderings Orderings of bundles by a fixed linear value vector. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 56
  14. Homothetic preferences Scaling two bundles by the same factor preserves their comparison. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 57
  15. Quasi-linear preferences Adding the same amount of the numeraire good to two bundles preserves their comparison. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 58 Corrected statement
  16. Quasi-linearity in all commodities Every good acts like money: adding a common amount of any good preserves comparisons. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 60
  17. Directions valued by subjective marginal values The directions with positive value under a vector of subjective marginal values. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 61
  18. Improvement directions The directions in which small moves make the consumer better off. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 62
  19. Differentiable preferences A preference is differentiable when its improvement directions are those with positive value under a marginal-value vector. Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 62
  20. Λ-convex preferences Richter and Rubinstein’s definition of convexity without algebra: b is at least as good as a whenever, by every criterion, b ranks at least as high as something at least as good as a. Richter and Rubinstein (2019), Definition 1, p. 1170

Results 15

  1. Utility representation of monotone continuous preferences Monotone and continuous preferences over bundles have a continuous utility representation. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 50
  2. Equivalence of the three definitions of convexity The three definitions of convex preferences coincide for every preference relation. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 53
  3. Convexity and quasi-concavity · Convexity and quasi-concave utility A represented preference is convex exactly when its utility function is quasi-concave. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 53
  4. Convex preferences need not have a concave utility Some continuous and convex preference on ℝ is represented by no concave function. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 54 Corrected statement
  5. Λ-convexity on finite sets: the bottom-up construction On a finite set, Λ-convex preferences are exactly those built by placing Λ-minimal alternatives one at a time. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 4, pp. 54–55
  6. Single-peaked preferences as Λ-strictly convex preferences On [0, 1], continuous single-peaked preferences are the Λ-strictly convex ones for the left and right criteria. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 55
  7. Convexity and Ψ-convexity Ψ-convexity, the separating-hyperplane form of convexity, implies convexity; the converse needs interior bundles. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 56 Corrected statement
  8. Homothetic utility · Utility representation of homothetic preferences Homothetic, continuous and monotone preferences have a continuous utility that is homogeneous of degree one. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 57
  9. Quasi-linear utility · Utility representation of quasi-linear preferences Monotone, continuous preferences that are quasi-linear and strongly monotone in good 1 have a utility equal to the amount of good 1 plus a function of the other goods. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 58
  10. Valuing a bundle in units of the numeraire For monotone, continuous preferences that are quasi-linear and strongly monotone in good 1, every bundle without good 1 is indifferent to some amount of good 1 alone. Lemma Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 59
  11. Linear utility from quasi-linearity in all goods Continuous, strongly monotone preferences that are quasi-linear in every good have a positive linear utility. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 60
  12. Properties of improvement directions Four basic facts about improvement directions under scaling, convexity and monotonicity. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 62
  13. Differentiable utility and differentiable preferences Stated in the text: a differentiable, strongly increasing, quasi-concave utility gives a differentiable preference; a machine-checked proof is still open. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 4, p. 63 Open
  14. Standard convexity as Ψ-convexity On a convex, closed subset of ℝᴺ, a continuous preference is convex in the usual sense exactly when it is Λ-convex for the linear orderings x ↦ v · x. Example Richter and Rubinstein (2019), Example 2, p. 1172
  15. Two definitions of Λ-convexity compared The textbook’s Λ-convexity, read with the strict parts of the orderings, implies Richter and Rubinstein’s; the converse does not hold in general. Theorem Richter and Rubinstein (2019)

Problems 10

In the practice room
  1. Problem 1. Strict convexity on the unit interval On the unit interval, strictly convex continuous preferences are the single-peaked ones. Problem Set 4 Easy
  2. Problem 2. Monotone, quasi-linear and homothetic preferences With two goods and no continuity, three classic properties pin preferences down to lexicographic rankings by linear indices. Problem Set 4 Difficult
  3. Problem 3. Convexity as a diminishing rate of substitution A discrete diminishing-rate-of-substitution condition is equivalent to convexity for strongly monotone, continuous preferences. Problem Set 4 Standard
  4. Problem 4. Linear preferences For continuous, strongly monotone preferences, quasi-linearity in every commodity forces a linear utility representation. Problem Set 4 Standard
  5. Problem 5. Quasi-linear preferences and the numeraire Under continuity and monotonicity, with quasi-linearity and strong monotonicity in good 1, every bundle can be priced in units of good 1. Problem Set 4 Difficult
  6. Problem 6. Separable preferences Additive utility implies independence across groups of goods and the hexagon condition, but independence alone is not enough for additivity. Problem Set 4 Easy 3 parts
  7. Problem 7. Differentiable preferences The book’s direction-based notion of differentiable preferences, tested on lexicographic, flat and discontinuous examples. Problem Set 4 Difficult 5 parts Corrected statement
  8. Problem 8. A convex but discontinuous preference on the line A preference on the real line that is convex and not continuous; its differentiability depends on whether values may be negative. Problem Set 4 Easy Corrected statement
  9. Problem 9. Λ-strictly convex preferences on the unit interval For the two monotone orderings of the unit interval, Λ-strict convexity is ordinary strict convexity. Problem Set 4 Easy
  10. Problem 10. Convexity through strict upper contour sets Convex preferences can be tested on strict upper contour sets, provided the set of alternatives is convex. Problem Set 4 Easy Corrected statement

  3. Problem 4. Preferences over portfolios of risky assets A risk-averse expected-utility investor’s ranking of asset holdings is convex, and monotone once there is at least one asset. Rubinstein, Problem Set 8, Problem 4, p. 125 Standard 2 parts Corrected statement

MIT 14.121, Problem Set 3, Question 1 Corrected statement
