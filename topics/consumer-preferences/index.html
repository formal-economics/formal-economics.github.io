Fields of economics/Part II · Consumers and producers

Consumer preferences

Preferences over bundles carry economic structure: monotonicity, continuity, convexity, homotheticity, quasi-linearity and differentiability. Each has a counterpart in utility, such as quasi-concavity for convexity and homogeneity for homotheticity.

Sources
Rubinstein Richter and Rubinstein (2019)

Results to know

The landmarks of the field, to read first.

  1. ≿ convex⟺u quasi-concave Claim Convexity and quasi-concavity A represented preference is convex exactly when its utility function is quasi-concave. Lecture 4, p. 53
  2. U(λx)=λU(x) Claim Homothetic utility Homothetic, continuous and monotone preferences have a continuous utility that is homogeneous of degree one. Lecture 4, p. 57
  3. u(x)=x1​+v(x2​,…,xK​) Claim Quasi-linear utility Monotone, continuous preferences that are quasi-linear and strongly monotone in good 1 have a utility equal to the amount of good 1 plus a function of the other goods. Lecture 4, p. 58

Definitions

  1. Monotone preferences More of every good is at least as good; strictly more of every good is strictly better. Lecture 4, p. 49
  2. Strongly monotone preferences Any increase in any good, keeping the others, is strictly better. Lecture 4, p. 49
  3. Nonsatiation Arbitrarily close to a bundle there is a strictly better one. Lecture 4, p. 49
  4. Continuity of consumer preferences Strict preferences between bundles survive small changes in either bundle. Lecture 4, p. 49
  5. Convex preferences: first definition Moving toward a weakly better bundle never makes things worse. Lecture 4, p. 52
  6. Convex preferences: second definition A mixture of two bundles is at least as good as one of them. Lecture 4, p. 52
  7. Convex preferences: third definition Upper contour sets are convex. Lecture 4, p. 52
  8. Strictly convex preferences Strict mixtures of distinct bundles that are at least as good as y are strictly better than y. Lecture 4, p. 53
  9. Concave and quasi-concave functions Not a formal statement A reminder of the mathematical definitions used in the link between convexity and utility. Lecture 4, p. 53
  10. Λ-convex preferences Convexity as the existence, at every alternative, of a criterion that must improve. Lecture 4, p. 54
  11. Λ-strictly convex preferences The strict version of Λ-convexity, covering moves to equally good alternatives. Lecture 4, p. 54
  12. Single-peaked preferences Preferences on an interval with a unique ideal point, declining on both sides. Lecture 4, p. 55
  13. Algebraic linear orderings Orderings of bundles by a fixed linear value vector. Lecture 4, p. 56
  14. Homothetic preferences Scaling two bundles by the same factor preserves their comparison. Lecture 4, p. 57
  15. Quasi-linear preferences Corrected Adding the same amount of the numeraire good to two bundles preserves their comparison. Lecture 4, p. 58
  16. Quasi-linearity in all commodities Every good acts like money: adding a common amount of any good preserves comparisons. Lecture 4, p. 60
  17. Directions valued by subjective marginal values The directions with positive value under a vector of subjective marginal values. Lecture 4, p. 61
  18. Improvement directions The directions in which small moves make the consumer better off. Lecture 4, p. 62
  19. Differentiable preferences A preference is differentiable when its improvement directions are those with positive value under a marginal-value vector. Lecture 4, p. 62
  20. Λ-convex preferences Richter and Rubinstein’s definition of convexity without algebra: b is at least as good as a whenever, by every criterion, b ranks at least as high as something at least as good as a. Richter and Rubinstein (2019), Definition 1, p. 1170

More results

  1. Utility representation of monotone continuous preferences Monotone and continuous preferences over bundles have a continuous utility representation. Claim Lecture 4, p. 50
  2. Equivalence of the three definitions of convexity The three definitions of convex preferences coincide for every preference relation. Claim Lecture 4, p. 53
  3. Convex preferences need not have a concave utility Corrected Some continuous and convex preference on ℝ is represented by no concave function. Claim Lecture 4, p. 54
  4. Λ-convexity on finite sets: the bottom-up construction On a finite set, Λ-convex preferences are exactly those built by placing Λ-minimal alternatives one at a time. Claim Lecture 4, pp. 54–55
  5. Single-peaked preferences as Λ-strictly convex preferences On [0, 1], continuous single-peaked preferences are the Λ-strictly convex ones for the left and right criteria. Claim Lecture 4, p. 55
  6. Convexity and Ψ-convexity Corrected Ψ-convexity, the separating-hyperplane form of convexity, implies convexity; the converse needs interior bundles. Claim Lecture 4, p. 56
  7. Valuing a bundle in units of the numeraire For monotone, continuous preferences that are quasi-linear and strongly monotone in good 1, every bundle without good 1 is indifferent to some amount of good 1 alone. Lemma Lecture 4, p. 59
  8. Linear utility from quasi-linearity in all goods Continuous, strongly monotone preferences that are quasi-linear in every good have a positive linear utility. Claim Lecture 4, p. 60
  9. Properties of improvement directions Four basic facts about improvement directions under scaling, convexity and monotonicity. Claim Lecture 4, p. 62
  10. Differentiable utility and differentiable preferences Open Stated in the text: a differentiable, strongly increasing, quasi-concave utility gives a differentiable preference; a machine-checked proof is still open. Claim Lecture 4, p. 63
  11. Standard convexity as Ψ-convexity On a convex, closed subset of ℝᴺ, a continuous preference is convex in the usual sense exactly when it is Λ-convex for the linear orderings x ↦ v · x. Example Richter and Rubinstein (2019), Example 2, p. 1172
  12. Two definitions of Λ-convexity compared The textbook’s Λ-convexity, read with the strict parts of the orderings, implies Richter and Rubinstein’s; the converse does not hold in general. Theorem Richter and Rubinstein (2019)

Also relevant, from other fields

  1. Question 3. Quasi-linear preferences have no wealth effects With preferences quasi-linear in the first good, demand for the other goods ignores wealth, Hicksian demand ignores the target utility, v = w + φ(p) and e = c(u) − φ(p). MIT 14.121, Problem Set 3
  2. Question 4. Demand for the utility max{ax, ay} + min{x, y} With u = max{ax, ay} + min{x, y}, indifference curves are kinked on the diagonal, and Marshallian and Hicksian demand pick a corner, the kink, or a segment by the price ratio. MIT 14.121, Problem Set 3
