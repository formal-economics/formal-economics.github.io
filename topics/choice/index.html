Choice and rationalizability

Choice functions record what a decision maker picks from each set of alternatives. The central question is rationalizability, whether the choices maximize some preference; conditions α and β and the weak axiom of revealed preference answer it.

Results to know

The landmarks of the field, to read first.

  1. α∧β⟺WA Claim The weak axiom (WARP) On an intersection-closed domain, conditions α and β together are equivalent to the weak axiom. Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 33
  2. Proposition Rationalizability by α and β If the domain contains every menu of at most three alternatives, a choice correspondence satisfying α and β is the set of best elements of a preference relation. Rubinstein, Lecture 3, pp. 33–34
  3. Claim Satisficing is rationalizable Satisficing choices can always be explained as maximization of some preference relation. Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 30
  4. Proposition Rationalizability of choice functions satisfying condition α If the domain contains every menu of at most three alternatives, every choice function satisfying condition α is rationalizable. Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 28

Definitions

  1. Choice problems and contexts Choices are observed on a domain of nonempty menus drawn from a grand set of alternatives. Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 23
  2. Choice function A choice function selects one alternative from every menu in its domain. Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 24
  3. Rational choice function The choice function that picks the best element of each menu according to a fixed preference. Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 25
  4. Rationalizable choice A choice function is rationalizable if it coincides with the rational choice function of some preference relation. Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 27
  5. Condition α Contraction consistency: an alternative chosen from a menu is still chosen from any smaller menu that contains it. Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 27
  6. Choice correspondence A choice correspondence selects a nonempty set of acceptable alternatives from every menu. Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 31
  7. Rational choice correspondence The set of best elements of each menu according to a fixed preference relation. Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 32
  8. Weak axiom of revealed preference (choice correspondences) Once x is revealed at least as good as y, y is never revealed strictly better than x. Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 32
  9. Condition α (choice correspondences) An alternative chosen from a menu remains chosen from any smaller menu that contains it. Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 33
  10. Condition β (choice correspondences) Alternatives tied at the top of a menu remain tied when the menu expands. Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 33
  11. Indirect preferences over menus Menus of alternatives are ranked by the best alternative they contain. Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 82
  12. Indirect utility of a menu The indirect utility of a menu is the utility of the alternative chosen from it. Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 82

Results

  1. Rational choice satisfies condition α Choice functions induced by preference relations satisfy condition α. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 27
  2. Rationalizability of choice functions satisfying condition α If the domain contains every menu of at most three alternatives, every choice function satisfying condition α is rationalizable. Proposition Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 28
  3. Rationalizability on union-closed domains On a domain closed under unions, condition α suffices for rationalizability. Proposition Rubinstein, Lecture 3, pp. 28–29
  4. Satisficing satisfies condition α The satisficing procedure produces choices consistent with condition α. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 29
  5. Satisficing is rationalizable Satisficing choices can always be explained as maximization of some preference relation. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 30
  6. Rationalizability by α and β · Rationalizable choice correspondences (conditions α and β) If the domain contains every menu of at most three alternatives, a choice correspondence satisfying α and β is the set of best elements of a preference relation. Proposition Rubinstein, Lecture 3, pp. 33–34
  7. The weak axiom (WARP) · Conditions α and β versus the weak axiom On an intersection-closed domain, conditions α and β together are equivalent to the weak axiom. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 33
  8. Indirect preferences are preference relations The indirect preference over menus is reflexive, complete and transitive. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 82
  9. Indirect utility represents indirect preferences The indirect utility function represents the indirect preference over menus. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 82
  10. Recovering choices from menu preferences If removing an alternative makes a menu strictly worse, that alternative is the one chosen. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 83

Problems

Exercises from the sources, with their answers checked by computer.

Practise these problems →
  1. Problem 1. Is the procedure rational? Five verbal decision procedures, each tested against the choice model and the rational man paradigm. Problem Set 3 Easy
  2. Problem 2. Path independence Corrected statement Rational choice is path independent; path independence gives condition α once every subset is a choice problem, but not rationalizability. Problem Set 3 Moderately difficult
  3. Problem 3. Three choice correspondences and the weak axiom Three correspondences built from medians and maxima, tested against the weak axiom of revealed preference. Problem Set 3 Easy
  4. Problem 4. Choosing from the most common class A procedure that picks the best member of the most frequent class is consistent on pairs but need not be rational. Problem Set 3 Moderately difficult
  5. Problem 5. Threshold procedures (Kalai, Rubinstein and Spiegler) Two procedures that switch between two criteria at a threshold: one is always rationalizable, the other need not be. Problem Set 3 Moderately difficult
  6. Problem 6. Choice from lists (Rubinstein and Salant) When alternatives arrive as a list, order and repetition can matter; Property I with Duplication Invariance brings back rational choice. Problem Set 3 Moderately difficult
  7. Problem 7. Lexicographically rational choice (Rubinstein and Salant) Choice guided by the most notable alternative on the menu is characterized by a reference-point property. Problem Set 3 Difficult
  8. Problem 8. Choice by rationales (Cherepanov, Feddersen and Sandroni) Choosing the best of the alternatives one can rationalize is characterized by a weak form of the weak axiom; the warm-glow procedure is an example. Problem Set 3 Difficult
  9. Problem 9. Luce’s choice axiom · Luce's choice axiom Corrected statement Path independence of choice probabilities forces the Luce model, in which each alternative is chosen with probability proportional to a fixed weight. Problem Set 3 Moderately difficult

Also relevant, from other fields

  1. Best and worst elements of finite sets Every nonempty finite set has a best and a worst element under a preference relation. Lemma Rubinstein, Lecture 2, pp. 13–14
  2. Satisficing (Simon’s procedure) A decision maker examines alternatives in a fixed order and takes the first satisfactory one. Definition Rubinstein, Lecture 3, pp. 29–30
  3. Choice with a default option Choice problems in which one available alternative is the default. Definition Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 38
  4. The default-bias procedure Maximize utility, but give the default a non-negative bonus. Definition Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 39
  5. Weak axiom for choice with defaults A revealed-preference consistency condition adapted to choice problems with a default. Definition Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 40
  6. Default tendency Promoting the chosen alternative to default does not change the choice. Definition Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 40
  7. Characterization of the default-bias procedure On a finite set, the weak axiom and default tendency characterize choice that maximizes utility with a bonus for the default. Proposition Rubinstein, Lecture 3, p. 40
  8. Revealed preference from demand What a rationalizing monotone preference must say about chosen and affordable bundles. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 5, p. 75
  9. Kannai–Peleg theorem · Ranking menus (Kannai and Peleg) Two natural axioms for ranking menus are independent and can hold together, but they clash as soon as three alternatives are strictly ranked. Problem Rubinstein, Problem Set 1, Problem 3, pp. 10–11
  10. A money pump · A money pump against someone who believes 2 + 3 = 6 A decision maker who systematically calculates 2 + 3 = 6 can be led through trades that return his holdings but take his money, without limit. Problem Rubinstein, Problem Set 7, Problem 7, p. 112
