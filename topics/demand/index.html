Fields of economics/Part II · Consumers and producers

Consumer demand and duality

The consumer chooses a best bundle in the budget set, and demand records that choice at every price and wealth. The entries run from Walras’ law and first-order conditions to Roy’s identity and the duality with expenditure minimization.

Results to know

The landmarks of the field, to read first.

  1. −∂v/∂w(p∗,w∗)∂v/∂pk​(p∗,w∗)​=xk​(p∗,w∗) Claim Roy’s identity Demand is recovered from the indirect utility function as minus the ratio of its price and wealth derivatives. Lecture 6, p. 85
  2. p⋅x=w Claim Walras’ law With monotone preferences, optimal bundles exhaust the budget. Lecture 5, p. 70
  3. (p′−p)⋅(x(p′,w′)−x(p,w))<0 Claim Slutsky’s compensated law of demand Under Walras’ law and the weak axiom, demand moves opposite to compensated price changes. Lecture 5, p. 78
  4. x∗ solves P(p,w∗)⟺w∗ solves D(p,x∗) Claim Consumer duality For monotone and continuous preferences, a bundle solves the prime problem exactly when it solves the corresponding dual problem. Lecture 6, p. 88
  5. ≿ continuous⇒max≿​B(p,w)=∅ Claim Existence of demand A continuous preference attains a best bundle in every budget set with positive prices and wealth. Lecture 5, p. 67
  6. ≿ strictly convex⇒at most one x∗ Claim Uniqueness of demand Convex preferences give a convex set of optimal bundles; strictly convex preferences give at most one. Lecture 5, p. 67

Definitions

  1. Indirect preferences over budget sets Prices and wealth are ranked by the bundles they allow the consumer to buy. Lecture 6, p. 83
  2. The prime problem and the demand Demand is the unique solution of utility maximization over the budget set. Lecture 6, p. 87
  3. Hicksian demand and the expenditure function The cheapest bundle at least as good as a target bundle, and its cost. Lecture 6, p. 87

More results

  1. First-order conditions for the consumer problem A consumed good must offer the highest subjective value per unit of price. Claim Lecture 5, p. 68
  2. MRS equals the price ratio · The marginal rate of substitution equals the price ratio For two consumed goods, the ratio of subjective values equals the ratio of prices. Claim Lecture 5, p. 69
  3. First-order conditions are sufficient under convexity For strongly monotone, convex, continuous and differentiable preferences, a bundle that exhausts the budget and meets the first-order conditions is optimal. Claim Lecture 5, p. 69
  4. Continuity of the demand function Continuous preferences with unique optima give demand that varies continuously with prices and wealth. Claim Lecture 5, p. 71
  5. Closed graph of the demand correspondence Limits of optimal bundles are optimal at the limiting prices and wealth. Claim Lecture 5, p. 71
  6. Revealed preference from demand What a rationalizing monotone preference must say about chosen and affordable bundles. Claim Lecture 5, p. 75
  7. Properties of indirect preferences over budget sets Indirect preferences over budget sets are invariant to scaling, weakly decreasing in prices, strictly increasing in wealth, continuous and quasi-convex. Claim Lecture 6, pp. 83–84
  8. The prime and dual turtle A one-dimensional illustration of duality: maximal distance in given time versus minimal time for a given distance. Claim Lecture 6, p. 86
  9. Properties of Hicksian demand and the expenditure function Homogeneity, the compensated law of demand, monotonicity, continuity and concavity of the expenditure function, and the dual of Roy’s identity. Claim Lecture 6, pp. 87–88

Lecture Notes in Microeconomic Theory

  1. Problem 1. Homothetic preferences and demand Homothetic preferences produce demand that is proportional to wealth. Problem Set 5 Easy
  2. Problem 2. Quasi-linear preferences and wealth effects For a consumer quasi-linear in the first commodity, demand for that commodity is zero up to a wealth threshold and then absorbs all further wealth, or is zero throughout. Problem Set 5 Standard
  3. Problem 3. The demand correspondence The set of all optimal bundles, computed for perfect substitutes and shown to be upper semi-continuous for continuous preferences. Problem Set 5 Moderately difficult
  4. Problem 4. Which behaviour patterns are rationalizable? Buying up to one unit of good 1 is fully rationalizable; choosing quantities in proportion to prices is not. Problem Set 5 Moderately difficult
  5. Problem 5. A consumer with a utility floor Corrected statement Securing a utility floor and then maximizing the amount of good 1 is rationalizable, spends all wealth and gives continuous demand; a closed floor set ensures a unique choice. Problem Set 5 Moderately difficult
Show every problem from RubinsteinShow fewer
  1. Problem 6. The representative consumer Aggregate demand need not behave like the demand of a single rational consumer. Problem Set 5 Moderately difficult
  2. Problem 7. Under the weak axiom, Giffen goods are inferior Corrected statement A good whose demand rises with its own price must, somewhere, be one whose demand falls with wealth — given consistent choice. Problem Set 5 Standard
  3. Problem 8. Strictly concave preferences Corrected statement Consumers who prefer extremes to mixtures choose corners, and their demand jumps at a threshold price. Problem Set 5 Standard
  4. Problem 9. Saving as a residual decision Corrected statement Saving what is left after pursuing an aspiration level is fully rationalizable where choice is unique, but for some value functions by no continuous or differentiable preference. Problem Set 5 Moderately difficult
  5. Problem 1. Is v = w/p₁ + w/p₂ a rational consumer’s indirect utility? Roy’s identity turns v = w/p₁ + w/p₂ into a demand; the utility √x₁ + √x₂ generates that demand with indirect utility √v, and its square recovers v exactly. Problem Set 6 Easy
  6. Problem 2. Continuity of the Hicksian demand Monotone, continuous and strictly convex preferences make the Hicksian demand h(p, z) continuous in prices and in the reference bundle. Problem Set 6 Moderately difficult
  7. Problem 3. Compensating and equivalent variation Corrected statement Two money measures of a price change: computed for perfect substitutes, compared with the area under demand, and equal for strictly convex quasi-linear preferences when only the first price moves. Problem Set 6 Moderately difficult
  8. Problem 4. The envelope theorem and Roy’s identity Corrected statement The envelope theorem holds once the Lagrangian and the solution are differentiable, and applied to the consumer’s problem it yields Roy’s identity. Problem Set 6 Moderately difficult

MIT 14.121 Microeconomic Theory I

  1. Question 1. CES demand and the elasticity of substitution Corrected statement CES demand and indirect utility, their linear and Leontief limits, and the elasticity of substitution: 1/(1 − ρ) for ρ ≠ 0, 1; 0 for Leontief; 1 for Cobb–Douglas; none for linear. Problem Set 3
  2. Question 2. What utility maximization implies for two demand functions Given demand formulas for two of three goods, Walras’ law yields the third, both formulas are homogeneous of degree zero, and utility maximization forces α = 100, β = γ = −5. Problem Set 3
  3. Question 3. Quasi-linear preferences have no wealth effects With preferences quasi-linear in the first good, demand for the other goods ignores wealth, Hicksian demand ignores the target utility, v = w + φ(p) and e = c(u) − φ(p). Problem Set 3
  4. Question 4. Demand for the utility max{ax, ay} + min{x, y} With u = max{ax, ay} + min{x, y}, indifference curves are kinked on the diagonal, and Marshallian and Hicksian demand pick a corner, the kink, or a segment by the price ratio. Problem Set 3
  5. Question 5. Renting or buying a home Informal A discussion question on modelling rented and purchased housing in a family’s utility function when house prices rise much faster than rents. Problem Set 3

  1. Question 1. Recovering utility from the expenditure function Corrected statement From e(p, u) = p₁p₂u/(p₁ + p₂) one recovers utility on the axes, each indifference curve x₂ = (√u₀ − √x₁)², and finally u = (√x₁ + √x₂)². Problem Set 4
  2. Question 2. An economy with no positive representative consumer With fixed wealth shares, aggregate demand need not be generated by any rational preference: two consumers with kinked utilities produce a violation of the weak axiom. Problem Set 4
  3. Question 1. How deadweight loss changes with the tax Corrected statement The derivatives in the tax of two deadweight-loss measures: for a locally nonsatiated consumer both are zero at t = 0; for t > 0 the measure at the old utility rises when compensated demand slopes strictly down at the taxed price, while the one at the new utility can fall. Problem Set 5
  4. Question 2. Substitutes in compensated demand Corrected statement Substitutability need not be symmetric or the same at all prices; with enough regularity every good has a substitute; coffee can raise or lower what one pays to keep tea. Problem Set 5
  5. Question 3. From indirect utility to welfare and price indexes From v = I/√(p₁p₂): expenditure ū√(p₁p₂), Hicksian demand, a compensating variation of −400 for a fourfold price rise, and Paasche 8/5 < 2 < Laspeyres 5/2. Problem Set 5

Also relevant, from other fields

  1. Indirect preferences over menus Menus of alternatives are ranked by the best alternative they contain. Definition Lecture 6, p. 82
  2. Indirect preferences are preference relations The indirect preference over menus is reflexive, complete and transitive. Claim Lecture 6, p. 82
  3. Indirect utility of a menu The indirect utility of a menu is the utility of the alternative chosen from it. Definition Lecture 6, p. 82
  4. Indirect utility represents indirect preferences The indirect utility function represents the indirect preference over menus. Claim Lecture 6, p. 82
  5. Recovering choices from menu preferences If removing an alternative makes a menu strictly worse, that alternative is the one chosen. Claim Lecture 6, p. 83
