Topics/Part II · Consumers and producers

Consumer demand and duality

The consumer chooses a best bundle in the budget set, and demand records that choice at every price and wealth. The entries run from Walras’ law and first-order conditions to Roy’s identity and the duality with expenditure minimization.

Definitions 3

  1. Indirect preferences over budget sets Prices and wealth are ranked by the bundles they allow the consumer to buy. Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 83
  2. The prime problem and the demand Demand is the unique solution of utility maximization over the budget set. Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 87
  3. Hicksian demand and the expenditure function The cheapest bundle at least as good as a target bundle, and its cost. Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 87

Results 15

  1. Existence of demand · Existence of a solution to the consumer problem A continuous preference attains a best bundle in every budget set with positive prices and wealth. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 5, p. 67
  2. Uniqueness of demand · Convexity and uniqueness of the consumer’s solution Convex preferences give a convex set of optimal bundles; strictly convex preferences give at most one. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 5, p. 67
  3. First-order conditions for the consumer problem A consumed good must offer the highest subjective value per unit of price. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 5, p. 68
  4. MRS equals the price ratio · The marginal rate of substitution equals the price ratio For two consumed goods, the ratio of subjective values equals the ratio of prices. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 5, p. 69
  5. First-order conditions are sufficient under convexity For strongly monotone, convex, continuous and differentiable preferences, a bundle that exhausts the budget and meets the first-order conditions is optimal. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 5, p. 69
  6. Walras’ law With monotone preferences, optimal bundles exhaust the budget. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 5, p. 70
  7. Continuity of the demand function Continuous preferences with unique optima give demand that varies continuously with prices and wealth. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 5, p. 71
  8. Closed graph of the demand correspondence Limits of optimal bundles are optimal at the limiting prices and wealth. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 5, p. 71
  9. Revealed preference from demand What a rationalizing monotone preference must say about chosen and affordable bundles. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 5, p. 75
  10. Slutsky’s compensated law of demand · The compensated law of demand Under Walras’ law and the weak axiom, demand moves opposite to compensated price changes. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 5, p. 78
  11. Properties of indirect preferences over budget sets Indirect preferences over budget sets are invariant to scaling, weakly decreasing in prices, strictly increasing in wealth, continuous and quasi-convex. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 6, pp. 83–84
  12. Roy’s identity Demand is recovered from the indirect utility function as minus the ratio of its price and wealth derivatives. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 85
  13. The prime and dual turtle A one-dimensional illustration of duality: maximal distance in given time versus minimal time for a given distance. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 86
  14. Properties of Hicksian demand and the expenditure function Homogeneity, the compensated law of demand, monotonicity, continuity and concavity of the expenditure function, and the dual of Roy’s identity. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 6, pp. 87–88
  15. Consumer duality · Duality of utility maximization and expenditure minimization For monotone and continuous preferences, a bundle solves the prime problem exactly when it solves the corresponding dual problem. Claim Rubinstein, Lecture 6, p. 88

Problems 24

In the practice room
  1. Problem 1. Homothetic preferences and demand Homothetic preferences produce demand that is proportional to wealth. Problem Set 5 Easy
  2. Problem 2. Quasi-linear preferences and wealth effects For a consumer quasi-linear in the first commodity, demand for that commodity is zero up to a wealth threshold and then absorbs all further wealth, or is zero throughout. Problem Set 5 Standard
  3. Problem 3. The demand correspondence The set of all optimal bundles, computed for perfect substitutes and shown to be upper semi-continuous for continuous preferences. Problem Set 5 Moderately difficult 2 parts
  4. Problem 4. Which behaviour patterns are rationalizable? Buying up to one unit of good 1 is fully rationalizable on its natural reading; choosing quantities in proportion to prices is not. Problem Set 5 Moderately difficult 2 parts
  5. Problem 5. A consumer with a utility floor Securing a utility floor and then maximizing the amount of good 1 is rationalizable, spends all wealth and gives continuous demand; a closed floor set ensures a unique choice. Problem Set 5 Moderately difficult 6 parts Corrected statement
  6. Problem 6. The representative consumer Aggregate demand need not behave like the demand of a single rational consumer. Problem Set 5 Moderately difficult
  7. Problem 7. Giffen goods are inferior · Under the weak axiom, Giffen goods are inferior A good whose demand rises with its own price must, somewhere, be one whose demand falls with wealth — given consistent choice. Problem Set 5 Standard Corrected statement
  8. Problem 8. Strictly concave preferences Consumers who prefer extremes to mixtures choose corners, and their demand jumps at a threshold price. Problem Set 5 Standard 2 parts Corrected statement
  9. Problem 9. Saving as a residual decision Saving what is left after pursuing an aspiration level is fully rationalizable where choice is unique, but for some value functions by no continuous or differentiable preference. Problem Set 5 Moderately difficult 3 parts Corrected statement
  10. Problem 1. Is v = w/p₁ + w/p₂ a rational consumer’s indirect utility? Roy’s identity turns v = w/p₁ + w/p₂ into a demand; the utility √x₁ + √x₂ generates that demand with indirect utility √v, and its square recovers v exactly. Problem Set 6 Easy 6 parts
  11. Problem 2. Continuity of the Hicksian demand Monotone, continuous and strictly convex preferences make the Hicksian demand h(p, z) continuous in prices and in the reference bundle. Problem Set 6 Moderately difficult
  12. Problem 3. Compensating and equivalent variation Two money measures of a price change: computed for perfect substitutes, compared with the area under demand, and equal for strictly convex quasi-linear preferences when only the first price moves. Problem Set 6 Moderately difficult 4 parts Corrected statement
  13. Problem 4. The envelope theorem · The envelope theorem and Roy’s identity The envelope theorem holds once the Lagrangian and the solution are differentiable, and applied to the consumer’s problem it yields Roy’s identity. Problem Set 6 Moderately difficult 2 parts Corrected statement
  14. Question 1. CES demand and the elasticity of substitution CES demand and indirect utility, their linear and Leontief limits, and the elasticity of substitution: 1/(1 − ρ) for ρ ≠ 0, 1; 0 for Leontief; 1 for Cobb–Douglas; none for linear. MIT 14.121 · Problem Set 3 Corrected statement
  15. Question 2. What utility maximization implies for two demand functions Given demand formulas for two of three goods, Walras’ law yields the third, both formulas are homogeneous of degree zero, and utility maximization forces α = 100, β = γ = −5. MIT 14.121 · Problem Set 3
  16. Question 3. Quasi-linear preferences have no wealth effects With preferences quasi-linear in the first good, demand for the other goods ignores wealth, Hicksian demand ignores the target utility, v = w + φ(p) and e = c(u) − φ(p). MIT 14.121 · Problem Set 3
  17. Question 4. Demand for the utility max{ax, ay} + min{x, y} With u = max{ax, ay} + min{x, y}, indifference curves are kinked on the diagonal, and Marshallian and Hicksian demand pick a corner, the kink, or a segment by the price ratio. MIT 14.121 · Problem Set 3
  18. Question 5. Renting or buying a home A discussion question on modelling rented and purchased housing in a family’s utility function when house prices rise much faster than rents. MIT 14.121 · Problem Set 3 Informal
  19. Question 1. Recovering utility from the expenditure function From e(p, u) = p₁p₂u/(p₁ + p₂) one recovers utility on the axes, each indifference curve x₂ = (√u₀ − √x₁)², and finally u = (√x₁ + √x₂)². MIT 14.121 · Problem Set 4 Corrected statement
  20. Question 2. An economy with no positive representative consumer With fixed wealth shares, aggregate demand need not be generated by any rational preference: two consumers with kinked utilities produce a violation of the weak axiom. MIT 14.121 · Problem Set 4
  21. Question 1. How deadweight loss changes with the tax The derivatives in the tax of two deadweight-loss measures: for a locally nonsatiated consumer both are zero at t = 0; for t > 0 the measure at the old utility rises when compensated demand slopes strictly down at the taxed price, while the one at the new utility can fall. MIT 14.121 · Problem Set 5 Corrected statement
  22. Question 2. Substitutes in compensated demand Substitutability need not be symmetric or the same at all prices; with enough regularity every good has a substitute; coffee can raise or lower what one pays to keep tea. MIT 14.121 · Problem Set 5 Corrected statement
  23. Question 3. From indirect utility to welfare and price indexes From v = I/√(p₁p₂): expenditure ū√(p₁p₂), Hicksian demand, a compensating variation of −400 for a fourfold price rise, and Paasche 8/5 < 2 < Laspeyres 5/2. MIT 14.121 · Problem Set 5
  24. Question 1. Demand systems and expected utility Cobb–Douglas demand; when a linear demand system is Slutsky symmetric and comes from a preference; a utility index that is not risk averse; rankings no expected utility allows. BU Qualifying Exam (2020) 5 parts
