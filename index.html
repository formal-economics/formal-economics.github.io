I Individual choice

  • Preferences Preference relations are the primitive of choice theory: complete and transitive rankings, from which strict preference and indifference are derived. The two classical definitions, by questionnaire and by relation, describe the same objects. 13 D01 · D11

    Preference relationEquivalence of the two definitions of preferences

  • Utility representation A utility function represents a preference when higher numbers mean better alternatives. Preferences on countable sets always have one, continuous preferences on connected subsets of Euclidean space have a continuous one (Debreu), and the lexicographic preference on the square has none. 20 D11 · D01

    Debreu’s representation theoremNo utility representation for the lexicographic preference on the unit square

  • Choice and rationalizability Choice functions record what a decision maker picks from each set of alternatives. The central question is rationalizability, whether the choices maximize some preference; conditions α and β and the weak axiom of revealed preference answer it. 31 D01 · D11

    Rationalizability of choice functions satisfying condition αRationalizable choice correspondences (conditions α and β)

II Consumers and producers

  • Consumer preferences Preferences over bundles carry economic structure: monotonicity, continuity, convexity, homotheticity, quasi-linearity and differentiability. Each has a counterpart in utility, such as quasi-concavity for convexity and homogeneity for homotheticity. 45 D11

    Convexity and quasi-concave utilityUtility representation of homothetic preferences

  • Consumer demand and duality The consumer chooses a best bundle in the budget set, and demand records that choice at every price and wealth. The entries run from Walras’ law and first-order conditions to Roy’s identity and the duality with expenditure minimization. 42 D11

    Roy’s identityDuality of utility maximization and expenditure minimization

  • Producers and the firm A producer is described by a technology, the set of feasible plans, or by a production function. Profit maximization gives the profit and supply functions, with Hotelling’s lemma, and cost minimization gives the cost function. 19 D21 · D24

    Hotelling’s lemma and the profit functionProperties of the supply function

III Uncertainty and risk

  • Choice under uncertainty Choice under uncertainty ranks lotteries over prizes. Preferences satisfying independence and continuity have an expected-utility representation (von Neumann–Morgenstern), and de Finetti’s theorem derives subjective probabilities from preferences over bets. 12 D81

    Expected utility theorem (von Neumann–Morgenstern)De Finetti’s theorem on subjective probability

  • Risk attitudes For monetary lotteries, attitudes toward risk are read off the vNM utility. On an interval of prizes, risk aversion is concavity and being more risk averse is a concave transformation; first-order stochastic dominance is the ranking that every expected-utility maximizer who prefers more money shares. 11 D81

    First-order stochastic dominanceRisk aversion and concave utility

IV Behavioral decision theory

  • Behavioral decision theory Some choice procedures depart from maximizing a single preference, or reach consistent choices by other routes. Examples include Simon’s satisficing, choice with a default and a bias toward it, choice from lists and Luce’s probabilistic choice. 11 D91 · D81

    Satisficing is rationalizableCharacterization of the default-bias procedure

V Collective choice

  • Social choice Social choice aggregates individual preferences into a social ranking or decision. With at least three alternatives, Arrow’s theorem leaves only dictatorships under Pareto and independence of irrelevant alternatives; related entries treat strategy-proofness, majority rule and single-peaked preferences. 12 D71 · D72

    Arrow’s impossibility theoremGibbard–Satterthwaite and related impossibility theorems
