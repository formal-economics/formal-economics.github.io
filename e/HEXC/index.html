Claim

Constant absolute risk aversion and exponential utility

Monotone, risk-averse preferences that are invariant to wealth have linear or exponential utility on the real line.

As printed, this statement does not hold in full generality; a corrected companion is proved.

1 As printed

Invariance to wealth forces exponential or linear utility#

Assume that the expected utility of u represents the preference ≿ on L(Z), Z⊆R, which is monotonic (a>b implies [a]≻[b]), risk averse, and invariant to wealth (w+p≿w+q holds or fails independently of w). Then u is exponential or linear: u(x)=a+bx, or u(x)=a−be−αx with α>0.

2 Counterexample

A utility invariant to wealth, neither linear nor exponential#

Let Z={−31​}∪{0,1,2,…} and u(x)=min{2−21−x, 3x}. Then u is strictly increasing and continuous, and the preference represented by the expected utility of u is monotonic, risk averse and invariant to wealth (for every wealth level that keeps the prizes in Z); yet on Z the function u is neither of the form a+bx nor of the form a−be−αx with α>0.

Further counterexamples show that the printed statement also does not hold under narrower readings of invariance to wealth, and with the conclusion widened to include u(x)=a+bcx. On one infinite prize set, other preferences fail invariance to wealth or risk aversion, so the hypotheses are not vacuous there.

Conclusion

The printed statement does not hold on some infinite prize set.

Intuition

On a sparse prize set, shifting a lottery by a wealth level usually moves it outside Z, so invariance to wealth constrains u only weakly; an isolated prize such as −31​ can carry a utility value that breaks the exponential pattern.
