Question 1

Increasing differences

Increasing differences survive a non-decreasing change of variable, and h(x − θ) has increasing differences exactly when h is concave.

01The problem

Hide every answer, then reveal them one part at a time.

The source states this problem in the parts below.

Machine-checked statement 1

A monotone change of variable preserves increasing differences#

Answer

Shown. In words: relabelling the choice variable in an order-preserving way does not destroy the complementarity between the choice and the parameter.

A function f(x,y) has increasing differences if, for all x≤x′, the difference f(x′,y)−f(x,y) is non-decreasing in y. Let g:R→R be non-decreasing and let f:R2→R have increasing differences. Then h(x,y)=f(g(x),y) has increasing differences: for all x≤x′, the difference

f(g(x′),y)−f(g(x),y)

is non-decreasing in y.

Intuition

If x≤x′ then g(x)≤g(x′), so the difference in question is one that increasing differences of f already controls. For comparative statics this means the conclusion that higher y favours higher choices does not depend on how the choice is measured.

Machine-checked statement 2

h(x − θ) has increasing differences exactly when h is concave#

Answer

Shown: increasing differences of h(x−θ) is equivalent to concavity of h.

A function f(x,y) has increasing differences if, for all x≤x′, the difference f(x′,y)−f(x,y) is non-decreasing in y. Let h:R→R be twice differentiable. Then f(x,θ)=h(x−θ) has increasing differences if and only if h is concave.

Assumptions and the Lean statement

Intuition

For x≤x′, the derivative in θ of h(x′−θ)−h(x−θ) is h′(x−θ)−h′(x′−θ), which is nonnegative for all such pairs exactly when h′ is non-increasing, that is, when h is concave.

    Let g:R→R be non-decreasing. Show that if f:R2→R satisfies increasing differences, then so does the composite function h(x,y)=f(g(x),y). Interpret this in words. Intuitively, why is this a desirable property when we are thinking about comparative statics?
