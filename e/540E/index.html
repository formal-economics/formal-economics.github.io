Problem 7

The two envelopes paradox

Amounts 2ⁿ and 2ⁿ⁺¹ are drawn with probability 2ⁿ/3ⁿ⁺¹; when envelopes are assigned by a fair coin, your friend’s envelope is always worth more in expectation.

Rubinstein, Problem Set 8, Problem 7, p. 126 Choice under uncertainty · JEL D81 Entry 540E Citec

01The problem

Hide every answer, then reveal them one part at a time.

A number 2n is chosen with probability 2n/3n+1, and the amounts 2n and 2n+1 are put into two envelopes. One envelope is chosen randomly and given to you; the other goes to your friend. Verify that, whatever the amount in your envelope, the expected amount in your friend’s envelope is larger, so that it seems worthwhile to switch even without opening your envelope. Try to resolve this paradoxical conclusion.

  1. 1 As printed

    Your friend’s envelope is always worth more#

    Answer

    As printed, this does not hold in full generality, because “chosen randomly” does not say with which probabilities the envelopes are assigned. If you receive the smaller amount with probability 1/3 at every n, then given 2 in your envelope your friend’s expected amount is 7/4<2. The corrected statement assigns the envelopes by a fair coin.

    A number n∈{0,1,2,…} is drawn with probability 2n/3n+1, and the amounts 2n and 2n+1 are put into two envelopes; one envelope goes to you and the other to your friend. As printed, the envelope you receive is “chosen randomly”, and whatever the amount in your envelope, the expected amount in your friend’s envelope is larger.

    This printed statement has no Lean proof of its own; the corrected statement below is the one machine-checked.

    Intuition

    The conditional expectation weighs the two ways of holding a given amount, as the smaller or as the larger one. How the envelopes are assigned changes those weights, and with them the answer.

    2 Corrected statement

    With a fair coin, your friend’s envelope is always worth more#

    Answer

    Verified: given any amount x in your envelope, your friend’s envelope holds strictly more in expectation.

    A number n∈{0,1,2,…} is drawn with probability 2n/3n+1, and the amounts 2n and 2n+1 are put into two envelopes; one envelope goes to you and the other to your friend. If each of the two envelopes is given to you with probability 1/2, independently of n, then, writing X and Y for the amounts in your envelope and in your friend’s, for every amount x your envelope can hold

    E[Y∣X=x]>x.

    As the problem states it

    1. 1 Added condition The envelopes are assigned by a fair coin: you receive each of the two with probability 1/2, whatever n is.

    The Lean statement below states these assumptions together.

    The definitions it is stated with

    Intuition

    Holding x=2m with m≥1, you are the larger envelope with probability 3/5 and the smaller with probability 2/5, so your friend expects (2/5)(2x)+(3/5)(x/2)=11x/10; holding 1, he surely has 2. The paradox dissolves once one notes that each envelope’s unconditional expected amount, ∑n​4n/(2⋅3n), is infinite.
