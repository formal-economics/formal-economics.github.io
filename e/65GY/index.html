Problem 1

Two procedural preferences over lotteries

Ranking lotteries by the size of their support violates both the independence and the continuity axioms, ranking them by their most likely prize can violate both, and each is exposed to framing.

The problem

Consider two preference relations over lotteries described in the lecture: ranking lotteries by the size of their support, and comparing their most likely prizes.

  1. (a)

    Check carefully whether they satisfy axiom I (independence) and axiom C (continuity).

    Machine-checked statement 1 of 4

    Ranking lotteries by support size violates independence#

    Answer

    No: with two or more prizes, the support-size preference fails independence. With a single prize there is only one lottery and the axiom holds trivially, which is why the condition is needed.

    Let Z be a finite set of prizes and let ≿ rank lotteries p,q∈L(Z) by the size of their support: p≿q if and only if ∣supp(p)∣≤∣supp(q)∣, where supp(p)={z:p(z)>0}. If Z has at least two prizes, ≿ violates independence, the requirement that p≿q if and only if αp⊕(1−α)r≿αq⊕(1−α)r, for all lotteries p,q,r and α∈(0,1).

    The 6 definitions it is stated with

    Intuition

    The sure lotteries [a] and [b] are equally good, both having one prize. Mixed with [a], the first stays [a] while the second becomes a lottery over two prizes, so the same mixture breaks the tie.

    Machine-checked statement 2 of 4

    Ranking lotteries by support size violates continuity#

    Answer

    No: with two or more prizes, the support-size preference fails continuity. With a single prize the axiom holds, which is why the condition is needed.

    Let Z be a finite set of prizes and let ≿ rank lotteries p,q∈L(Z) by the size of their support: p≿q if and only if ∣supp(p)∣≤∣supp(q)∣, where supp(p)={z:p(z)>0}. If Z has at least two prizes, ≿ violates continuity, the requirement that whenever p≻q, every p′ close to p and every q′ close to q satisfy p′≻q′.

    Assumptions and the Lean statement

    Intuition

    A sure lottery [a] is strictly better than any lottery with two prizes, yet arbitrarily close to [a] lie lotteries that put a tiny probability on another prize. Their support jumps from one prize to two, so the strict preference cannot survive small perturbations.
