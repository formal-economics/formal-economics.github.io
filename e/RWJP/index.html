Kreps's definition of preferences

Taking strict preference as the primitive, with asymmetry and negative transitivity, is equivalent to the traditional definition.

The problem

Kreps (1990) takes as primitive a relation P, read “strictly preferred”, and requires asymmetry (for no x,y both xPy and yPx) and negative transitivity (if xPy, then for any z, xPz or zPy). Explain the sense in which this formalization is equivalent to the traditional definition of a preference relation.

Machine-checked statement

Answer

Proved: both translations, and both round trips, hold pointwise for all x,y. The two primitives carry exactly the same information.

Call a relation P on a set X a Kreps preference if it is asymmetric (never both xPy and yPx) and negatively transitive (if xPy, then for every z, xPz or zPy).

  1. If ≿ is a preference relation, its strict part, x≻y⟺(x≿y and not y≿x), is a Kreps preference.
  2. If P is a Kreps preference, the relation x≿y⟺¬(yPx) is a preference relation: reflexive, complete and transitive.
  3. The two translations are mutually inverse: from ≿ to its strict part and back returns ≿, and from P to ≿ and back to the strict part returns P.

The 4 definitions it is stated with

Intuition

“Weakly preferred” is just “not strictly worse”. Asymmetry of P turns into completeness and reflexivity of ≿, and negative transitivity of P is the contrapositive of transitivity of ≿.
