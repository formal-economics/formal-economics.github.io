Question 3

Skill and the choice of technology

Increasing differences make computer users buy more skill, and nothing weaker works for every cost of skill; as computers get cheaper, selected skill can jump.

Problem Producers and the firm MIT 14.121

The problem

Suppose there are two inputs to production, technology and the skill level of a single worker who uses the technology. With the computer technology the production function is G(s); with the old-fashioned technology it is H(s). The total cost of skill is c(s) on the market.

Machine-checked statement 1 of 5

Increasing differences make computer users buy more skill#

Answer · in our words

Our answer: a sufficient condition is that G−H is non-decreasing in skill, so that G and H have increasing differences. It works for every cost of skill and every set of skill levels.

Let G and H be the production functions of the computer and the old-fashioned technology, and c the total cost of skill, all functions of the skill level s. The pair has increasing differences in skill if, whenever s≤s′,

H(s′)−H(s)≤G(s′)−G(s),

that is, if G−H is non-decreasing. If G and H have increasing differences in skill then, for every cost of skill c and every set S of available skill levels, the largest maximizer of H(s)−c(s) on S is at most the largest maximizer of G(s)−c(s) on S, whenever both exist.

The definition it is stated with

Intuition

Moving from s to a higher s′ adds at least as much output with computers as with the old technology, while the cost of skill is the same for both. Whatever extra skill is worth buying with the old technology is therefore worth buying with computers.

Machine-checked statement 2 of 5

No weaker condition works for every cost of skill: first reading#

Answer · in our words

Our answer: no. If the result must hold for every cost of skill, increasing differences cannot be weakened.

Let G and H be the production functions of the computer and the old-fashioned technology, and c the total cost of skill, all functions of the skill level s. The pair has increasing differences in skill if, whenever s≤s′,

H(s′)−H(s)≤G(s′)−G(s),

that is, if G−H is non-decreasing. Call a condition on pairs (G,H) sufficient if, for every pair meeting it, every cost of skill c and every set S of available skill levels, the largest maximizer of H−c on S is at most the largest maximizer of G−c on S, whenever both exist. On our first reading of “weaker”: no sufficient condition is implied by increasing differences and also holds at some pair without increasing differences.

The Lean statement

Intuition

If G gains less than H between two skill levels s<s′, offer only these two levels and set the extra cost of s′ between the two gains: the old technology then buys s′ and computers buy s.
