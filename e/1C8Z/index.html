Preference reversal

Many people choose the safer of two bets yet put a higher money value on the riskier one: a discussion of the preference-reversal phenomenon.

After Slovic and Lichtenstein (1968)

Problem Behavioral decision theory Rubinstein

01The problem

Based on Slovic and Lichtenstein (1968). Let L1​=(8/9)[$4]⊕(1/9)[$0] and L2​=(1/9)[$40]⊕(8/9)[$0]. Discuss the phenomenon in which many people prefer L1​ to L2​ but, when asked for the certainty equivalents of the two lotteries, attach a lower value to L1​ than to L2​.

Not a formal statement

The problem asks for a discussion of the preference-reversal phenomenon for L1​=(8/9)[$4]⊕(1/9)[$0] and L2​=(1/9)[$40]⊕(8/9)[$0]. It contains no formal statement to check.

Intuition

If a decision maker had one preference over lotteries and sure amounts, monotone in money, the lottery he chooses could not have the lower certainty equivalent. The reversal suggests that choosing and pricing engage different procedures: pricing anchors on the large prize of L2​.
