MIT 14.121 Microeconomic Theory I

Glenn Ellison · 2005

MIT OpenCourseWare

ocw.mit.edu/courses/14-121-microeconomic-theory-i-fall-2015

22entries
22problems

Contents

  1. 1

    Problem Set 1

    4 questions

    • XF6A Indifference and strict preference Question 1 Machine-checked For a complete, reflexive and transitive preference, indifference is an equivalence relation, and strict preference followed by weak preference is strict.
    • NEFW Utility is ordinal Question 2 Machine-checked Any strictly increasing transformation of a utility function represents the same preferences.
    • KFSX Lexicographic preferences have no utility representation Question 3 Machine-checked Lexicographic preferences on ℝ² are complete, reflexive and transitive, yet no utility function represents them; of the standard axioms they violate only continuity.
    • WPRG Do survey answers reveal preferences? Question 4 Informal A discussion question on contingent valuation: which preference axioms might survey answers violate, and how could a survey test for such violations?
  2. 2

    Problem Set 2

    4 questions

    • 3Y6Y How much insurance to buy Question 1 Machine-checked A strictly risk-averse consumer insures fully when insurance is actuarially fair; when it is loaded and utility still rises at the insured wealth, she insures at most fully.
    • 185R To feed the parking meter or not Question 2 Corrected Paying a meter or risking a fine: elasticities of the gain from paying, a wealth threshold between payers and violators, and how fines, prices and policing move it.
    • ZSF9 The betweenness axiom Question 3 Machine-checked Betweenness follows from independence, means straight but not necessarily parallel indifference lines, and is compatible with the Allais paradox.
    • AD12 Should the young hold more stocks? Question 4 Informal A discussion question on the advice that younger people should invest a larger share of wealth in stocks than older people.
  3. 3

    Problem Set 3

    5 questions

    • S9ZZ CES demand and the elasticity of substitution Question 1 Corrected CES demand and indirect utility, their linear and Leontief limits, and the elasticity of substitution: 1/(1 − ρ) for ρ ≠ 0, 1; 0 for Leontief; 1 for Cobb–Douglas; none for linear.
    • WPSR What utility maximization implies for two demand functions Question 2 Machine-checked Given demand formulas for two of three goods, Walras’ law yields the third, both formulas are homogeneous of degree zero, and utility maximization forces α = 100, β = γ = −5.
    • EQWH Quasi-linear preferences have no wealth effects Question 3 Machine-checked With preferences quasi-linear in the first good, demand for the other goods ignores wealth, Hicksian demand ignores the target utility, v = w + φ(p) and e = c(u) − φ(p).
    • EN4K Demand for the utility max{ax, ay} + min{x, y} Question 4 Machine-checked With u = max{ax, ay} + min{x, y}, indifference curves are kinked on the diagonal, and Marshallian and Hicksian demand pick a corner, the kink, or a segment by the price ratio.
    • 85D0 Renting or buying a home Question 5 Informal A discussion question on modelling rented and purchased housing in a family’s utility function when house prices rise much faster than rents.
  4. 4

    Problem Set 4

    2 questions

    • ETHS Recovering utility from the expenditure function Question 1 Corrected From e(p, u) = p₁p₂u/(p₁ + p₂) one recovers utility on the axes, each indifference curve x₂ = (√u₀ − √x₁)², and finally u = (√x₁ + √x₂)².
    • ZTWY An economy with no positive representative consumer Question 2 Machine-checked With fixed wealth shares, aggregate demand need not be generated by any rational preference: two consumers with kinked utilities produce a violation of the weak axiom.
  5. 5

    Problem Set 5

    4 questions

    • B245 How deadweight loss changes with the tax Question 1 Corrected The derivatives in the tax of two deadweight-loss measures: both are zero at t = 0; for t > 0 the measure at the old utility rises, while the one at the new utility can fall.
    • ZFCH Substitutes in compensated demand Question 2 Corrected Substitutability need not be symmetric or the same at all prices; with enough regularity every good has a substitute; coffee can raise or lower what one pays to keep tea.
    • 4C7H From indirect utility to welfare and price indexes Question 3 Machine-checked From v = I/√(p₁p₂): expenditure ū√(p₁p₂), Hicksian demand, a compensating variation of −400 for a fourfold price rise, and Paasche 8/5 < 2 < Laspeyres 5/2.
    • NQ92 A monopolist’s output falls with marginal cost Question 4 Machine-checked For any demand curve whatever, a profit-maximizing monopolist with constant unit cost c produces weakly less when c is higher: a pure revealed-preference argument.
  6. 6

    Problem Set 6

    3 questions

    • VHE7 Increasing differences Question 1 Machine-checked Increasing differences survive a non-decreasing change of variable, and h(x − θ) has increasing differences exactly when h is concave.
    • DYEK Pricing against a shifting demand curve Question 2 Corrected A firm facing demand Q(p, θ) raises its price with θ when log Q has increasing differences, that is, when demand grows more inelastic; concave demand is not necessary for this.
    • C2MD Skill and the choice of technology Question 3 Machine-checked Increasing differences make computer users buy more skill, and nothing weaker works for every cost of skill; as computers get cheaper, selected skill can jump.
