Bayesian Persuasion

How a sender who decides what a Bayesian receiver will learn should design that information to move the receiver’s action.

By
Emir Kamenica and Matthew Gentzkow 2011
Published
American Economic Review 101(6), October 2011, pp. 2590–2615
In the library
In progress Last worked on 22 October 2026
Cite
BibTeX for the source

About the paper

A sender chooses a signal, a rule that maps the state of the world to messages; a receiver who updates by Bayes’ rule sees the message and acts. Choosing a signal is the same as choosing a distribution of posterior beliefs that averages to the prior, so the most the sender can get is the concave closure of its payoff, as a function of the receiver’s belief, evaluated at the prior.

  1. 1 Bayes plausibilityAny distribution of posterior beliefs whose average is the prior can be induced by some signal.
  2. 2 ConcavificationThe sender’s best expected payoff is the concave closure of its payoff over beliefs, at the prior; persuasion pays exactly when, at the prior, the closure lies above the payoff.
  3. 3 The prosecutorA judge convicts when guilt is at least as likely as not, and the prior probability of guilt is 0.3; the best investigation makes the judge convict 60% of the time.
